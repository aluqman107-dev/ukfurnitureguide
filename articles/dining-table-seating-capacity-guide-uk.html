How Many Chairs Fit Your Dining Table? UK Seating Guide (2026)

Updated for 2026 · Independent advice — nobody pays for a place in this guide.

In short: allow about 60cm of table edge per person. That means a 120cm table seats 4, a 150cm table seats 4–6, a 180cm table seats 6 (8 at a squeeze), and a 150cm round table seats 6. Leave 90cm of clearance between the table edge and the wall or nearest furniture. Not sure about your table? Try our free dining seats calculator — it works it out in seconds.

Buying a dining set and then realising the chairs don't fit is one of the most common — and most avoidable — furniture mistakes in the UK. Retailers sell "6-seater dining tables" by counting the chairs in the box, not by measuring whether six adults can actually eat at them. A 150cm table with six chairs wedged two per side plus the ends is a very different Christmas dinner from six people with proper elbow room.

A dining table's seating capacity is simply the number of people who can sit at it with roughly 60cm of table edge each — enough for a place setting, a glass, and room to move their arms without conducting someone else's cutlery. That single measurement answers nearly every question in this guide, because every capacity number below comes straight from it.

This guide gives you the 60cm-per-person rule, capacity charts for rectangular, round, square and extendable tables, and the clearance maths British dining rooms actually need. How many chairs fit a dining table?

Count 60cm of table edge per person and divide your table's length by it. A 120cm table seats 4, a 150cm table seats 4–6, a 180cm table seats 6, and 220cm and above seats 8. For round tables, a 120cm diameter seats 4 and a 150cm diameter seats 6. The detailed charts further down cover every common size.

That division is the honest version of the numbers printed on the box. When a listing says "6-seater", it usually means six chairs were photographed around it — not that six adults got 60cm each. Always measure the table length and divide by 60 yourself. A 150cm table labelled "6-seater" (150 ÷ 60 = 2.5 per side) can only seat six if the two end positions join in, which means someone sits at the head and foot with no one opposite them at all.

The other thing the box never tells you: the rule assumes people sit on the long sides. On a rectangular table the two ends are bonus seats, not the plan — though for Christmas or Eid, when the family doubles, those end seats save the day. This is why so many UK households end up with an extendable dining table: the day-to-day capacity and the celebration capacity are two different numbers, and extendables handle both.

How much space does each person need at a dining table?

Allow 60cm of table edge per person as the standard — enough for a dinner plate, side plate, glass and elbow room. In a tight everyday space, 55cm works for quick breakfasts but feels cramped over a long Sunday roast. For formal dining with full place settings and serving dishes in the middle, plan on 70cm per person.

Those numbers are wider than most dining chairs, and that is deliberate. A standard dining side chair is only about 45–50cm wide — the extra centimetres are elbow room and the personal space that stops neighbours bumping glasses. The chair fits; the person needs more than the chair.

Chair widths vs. the space people need

  • Standard dining side chair: about 45–50cm wide, but each diner still needs 60cm of table edge.
  • Dining armchair: about 60–65cm+ wide — budget 70cm of table edge per armchair.
  • Bench seating: measure 55–60cm per person; benches can flex because there's no armrest gap.
  • Bulkier carver chairs at the heads: they need 65–70cm of width on the table, plus clearance to push back.

One more wrinkle worth knowing: the 60cm rule measures edge space, not leg space. A table with chunky corner legs can meet the rule on paper while two diners' knees argue underneath. Pedestal bases and trestle ends seat more flexibly than four corner legs — nothing blocks your shins, so the end positions become genuinely usable rather than nominal.

How many chairs fit a 180cm dining table?

A 180cm dining table seats six people comfortably — three along each long side with 60cm of elbow room each (180 ÷ 60 = 3 per side). For occasional use you can seat eight by adding a chair at each end, provided the table is around 90–100cm wide so the end diners' knees don't clash with the side diners'.

The 180cm six-seater is the UK's most popular dining table size for a reason: it handles a family of four daily, with room to host, and still fits in a typical dining end of an open-plan kitchen. Here's how the other common lengths stack up:

Width matters almost as much as length. The standard UK dining table width is around 100cm, which leaves room for serving dishes between two rows of place settings. A 75cm-wide table still seats people by the 60cm rule, but dinner parties get logistically tricky — there is nowhere for the serving bowls to go. If you host properly rather than theoretically, treat width as part of the capacity question. How many chairs fit a round dining table?

A 120cm round dining table seats 4 comfortably, and a 150cm round table seats 6. On a round table nobody gets a bad seat — everyone faces the middle — but curves eat usable edge, so a round table needs slightly more diameter than the raw 60cm rule suggests to match a rectangle's capacity.

Round tables shine in smaller rooms because there are no corners to walk into and no "head" seat awkwardness when it's just two or three of you. The trade-off is conversation distance: at a 150cm round table, opposite diners are 150cm apart, which is fine; much beyond that and you are raising your voice over the candles.

Round tables and armchairs

A 6-seat round table needs about 150cm of diameter with armchairs — noticeably more than with slim side chairs. Each armchair wants roughly 70cm of edge, and the curve means the inner chair corners crowd each other. If you love armchairs and host six, measure for a 150cm round or go rectangular; a 120cm round with six armchairs is a geometry problem nobody wins.

Do extendable tables seat more people?

Yes — that's the whole point of buying one. A typical UK extendable seats 4 closed and 6 to 8 open, but exact numbers depend on the closed and extended lengths, so measure both against the 60cm-per-person rule. The catch is floor space: you need the full 90cm of clearance in the open position too, not just the closed one.

Extendables are the honest answer to the British dining dilemma: the room fits a 120cm table daily, but Christmas needs 180cm. The most common mechanisms are butterfly leaves (folding panels stored under the tabletop), pull-out end extensions, and drop-leaf tables, which hinge the sides down — see our drop-leaf vs extendable comparison for which mechanism suits small rooms best.

Three things to check before buying: first, that the extended length genuinely adds seats (a 120cm table extending to 150cm goes from 4 to 4–6 — real, but modest). Second, that the base supports the open table without wobble when six people lean on it. Third — the one everyone forgets — that the extension leaves have somewhere sensible to live, because a butterfly panel leaning against the garage wall gets damaged. How much clearance do you need around a dining table?

Allow 90cm from the table edge to the wall or nearest furniture — that's the minimum for someone to push their chair back and stand up, and for another person to walk past behind them. If people regularly walk behind a seated diner, as in a busy kitchen-diner, 120cm or more works far better.

Work it through with real numbers. A dining chair pulled out occupies roughly 60cm of floor depth; the other 30cm is the walking lane. Now take a typical 180cm × 90cm six-seater: it needs 90cm of clearance on all four sides, so the room must be at least 360cm × 270cm (3.6m × 2.7m) for comfortable use. That is bigger than many UK dining areas, which is exactly why measuring the room comes before choosing the table — not after.

The clearance cheat sheet

  • 90cm — minimum table-edge to wall/furniture, for pushing back and passing.
  • 120cm+ — when someone walks behind a seated diner regularly.
  • 60cm — the floor depth a pulled-out chair roughly occupies.
  • 150cm+ — if the table sits in a main walkway, not a dead-end dining zone.

Doors and radiators join the calculation. A door swinging into the clearance zone effectively deletes it, and a radiator under the window is a hard boundary — chairs can pull back over it only until someone's back hits the wall. Sketch the room, mark the doors, radiators and walkways, then lay the table footprint plus 90cm on top. If it overlaps, the table is too big for the room — and no amount of chair squeezing fixes it. Dining table seating FAQs

How much room does each person need at a dining table?

Allow 60cm of table edge per person for comfortable dining. That's enough for a dinner plate, side plate, glass and elbow room. In a tight everyday space 55cm per person works for breakfasts and quick meals, while formal dining with full place settings and serving dishes in the middle calls for about 70cm per person.

How many chairs fit a 180cm dining table?

A 180cm dining table seats six people comfortably — three along each long side with 60cm of elbow room each. For occasional use you can seat eight by adding a chair at each end, provided the table is around 90 to 100cm wide so the end diners' knees don't clash with the side diners.

Can you put 8 chairs at a 150cm dining table?

Not comfortably. A 150cm table seats four to six people — two per side plus one at each end. Eight diners need at least 200 to 220cm of table edge between them to keep 60cm of personal space each, so 150cm with eight chairs means cramped elbows and shared plates for everyone.

How much clearance do you need behind dining chairs?

Allow at least 90cm from the table edge to the wall or nearest furniture. That's the minimum for someone to push their chair back and walk past comfortably. If people regularly walk behind a seated diner — common in a busy kitchen-diner — plan for 120cm or more.

Do armchairs fit at a dining table?

Yes, but they need more room than standard dining chairs. Allow about 70cm of table edge per armchair instead of 60cm, and add roughly 10cm to the 90cm clearance allowance. On a 120cm round table, six armchairs typically need a table around 150cm across.
