Best Mattress for a Bunk Bed (UK 2026): The 15cm Rule and What to Buy

Buying a mattress for a bunk bed feels like it should be simple. It is a single bed, so you buy a single mattress — except it is not that simple, and the difference is a safety issue, not a comfort one. A standard single mattress is 20 to 25cm deep, and on the top bunk of a bunk bed that depth eats into the guard rail — the barrier that stops the sleeper rolling out in the night. Put a deep mattress on a top bunk and the rail that was meant to protect the sleeper ends up barely higher than the mattress, which is how children fall out of bunk beds. Get the depth right and the rest of the decision — foam or springs, firm or soft — is the same as buying any mattress.

What is the best mattress for a bunk bed?

A medium-firm single mattress no deeper than 15cm, in memory foam or pocket springs depending on the sleeper. Foam is quieter and lighter, which suits a top bunk; pocket springs sleep cooler and give more bounce. Either way, the depth matters more than the brand — a mattress that is too deep is a safety problem, not a comfort choice.

The best bunk bed mattress is the one that fits the bunk, not the one with the best reviews. Start with the depth — 15cm or less on the top bunk — then the size, which is a standard UK single at 90 by 190cm for almost every bunk sold in Britain, then the firmness, which should be medium to medium-firm for most children. Only then does the type matter. Memory foam contours to the body, holds its shape, and is the quietest option there is — nobody underneath hears the sleeper turn over — and it is light, which matters when you are hauling a mattress up a ladder. Pocket springs give better airflow, so they sleep cooler, and a bouncier, more responsive feel that some children prefer. Open-coil springs are the cheapest option and fine for a bottom bunk or a guest bed, but they can squeak, and on a bunk bed a squeak carries through the frame at night. The mattress that wins is the one that is safe first and comfortable second, in that order — which is why this guide starts with the depth rule and not with a product list.

BS EN 747 — the bunk bed safety standard

UK bunk beds are built to BS EN 747, which requires the guard rail to stand at least 16cm above the top of the mattress on the upper bunk, and the top bunk only for children aged 6 and over. The mattress must fit the base without gaps at the sides. If your bunk has a maximum-mattress mark, that number is the law for your bed — never a general figure from a guide.

How thick should a bunk bed mattress be?

No deeper than 15cm on the top bunk — that is the figure Dreams and Silentnight both give, and it is what keeps the sleeper safely below the guard rail. Some bunks allow 16cm, like the Simba Hybrid Kids mattress, but only where the bed's own maximum-mattress mark says so. Never guess: check the mark on your bunk.

The 15cm rule is the single most important number in this guide, and it is worth understanding why it exists. The guard rail on a top bunk has to stand at least 16cm above the mattress surface — that is the BS EN 747 requirement — so that a sleeping child cannot roll over it. A 15cm mattress leaves the rail standing proud by a safe margin; a 20cm mattress eats 5cm of that margin, and a 25cm mattress eats 10cm, leaving a rail that is barely a lip. Dreams states that bunk mattresses should be no deeper than 15cm, and Silentnight says a top-bunk mattress must not exceed 15cm in depth — and both are right, because the margin is the safety. The one exception proves the rule: the Simba Hybrid Kids mattress is made at 16cm deep, and Simba sells it for bunks only where the bed's maximum-mattress mark allows 16cm — buy it for a bunk marked 15cm and you have broken the rule by a centimetre. The bottom bunk is different: there is no guard-rail rule below, so a deeper mattress is fine there, and many families put the thicker, more comfortable mattress on the bottom and the slim one on top. Check the mark on your bunk before you order — it is usually a label on the frame or a line in the instructions — and measure the mattress you already own before assuming it fits.

What size mattress does a bunk bed take?

Two standard UK singles, 90 by 190cm, for almost every bunk bed sold in Britain. The width and length are the same as a normal single bed — only the depth is different. Some bunks take a small double on the bottom, so check both bunks separately before you order, and measure the base for gaps.

Size is the easy part, and it is the part people get wrong least — but there are two catches worth naming. The first is that both bunks usually take the same size, a standard UK single at 90 by 190cm, but not always: triple bunks and L-shaped bunks sometimes take a small double at 120 by 190cm on the bottom, and some continental bunks take a 90 by 200cm single, so check each bunk separately rather than ordering two of the same. The second catch is the fit: a bunk mattress must fill the base without a gap at the sides, because a gap is a trap — a child's arm or leg can slip into it, and on the top bunk a gap at the guard rail is a fall risk. Measure the base, not the label: the frame's internal measurements are what matter, and a mattress that is a few centimetres short on each side is a mattress that does not fit. And check the weight as well as the size: a lighter mattress is easier to lift up a ladder and easier to turn, and on a top bunk that matters more than it does on a normal bed, where you can walk around all four sides.

Memory foam or pocket sprung for a bunk bed?

Memory foam for the top bunk, pocket springs if the sleeper runs hot. Foam is quieter — nobody underneath hears the sleeper turn over — lighter to haul up a ladder, and it holds its shape. Pocket springs give better airflow and a bouncier feel, but they are heavier and can squeak, which matters more when someone is sleeping below.

The foam-versus-springs choice on a bunk bed is really a choice about who sleeps underneath. Memory foam is the quietest mattress there is: it absorbs movement instead of passing it on, so the child on the top bunk can turn over without the child below feeling it, and it makes no noise at all when the sleeper moves. It is also lighter than a sprung mattress of the same size, which matters twice over — once when you are carrying it up the ladder, and again every time you change the sheets. The trade-off is heat: foam holds warmth, and on a top bunk, where warm air rises and the ceiling is close, a hot sleeper can wake up sweaty. Pocket springs solve the heat problem, because the springs leave air gaps inside the mattress and heat escapes instead of building up, and they give a bouncier, more responsive feel that suits children who like to bounce. But they are heavier, they can squeak as the springs age, and a squeak on a bunk bed carries through the frame. Open-coil springs are the budget option: cheap, light enough, and fine for a bottom bunk or a guest bed, but they are the squeakiest of the three and the least supportive over time. What firmness should a bunk bed mattress be?

Medium to medium-firm suits most children. Too soft and the hips sink, curving the spine; too firm and the shoulders and hips take the pressure. A medium-firm mattress supports a growing child without feeling like a board, and it is the firmness most bunk-specific mattresses are made in.

Firmness on a bunk bed matters more than on a normal bed, because the sleeper is usually a child whose body is still growing, and a mattress that is too soft or too firm does more than disturb sleep — it shapes posture night after night. Medium to medium-firm is the sweet spot for most children: firm enough that the hips do not sink and the spine stays straight, soft enough that the shoulders and hips are cushioned rather than pressed. Too soft, and the hips sink while the shoulders stay high, curving the spine; too firm, and the pressure lands on the shoulders and hips, which is what makes a child toss and turn. The firmness scale runs from 1 to 10, and a medium-firm bunk mattress usually sits around 6 or 7 — the same range most adult mattresses aim for, which is no coincidence. If you are unsure, use the weight rule: lighter children suit medium, heavier children and teenagers suit medium-firm, and anyone who sleeps on their side needs a touch more give at the shoulder than a back sleeper does. Can adults sleep on a bunk bed mattress?

Yes, on an adult-rated bunk — and the mattress matters less than the frame. Check the bunk's weight limit first: many children's bunks are rated to 76kg per bunk, while adult bunks are built heavier. Then choose a 15cm mattress in the sleeper's preferred firmness, remembering the depth rule still applies on the top bunk.

Adults sleep on bunk beds more often than the children's-furniture label suggests — in shared flats, in holiday lets, in rooms where two adults need two beds in the floor space of one — and the mattress question is the same, with one extra check. The frame's weight limit comes first: many children's bunks are rated to 76kg, or 12 stone, per bunk, which covers most children and lighter adults but not a heavier adult, while adult-rated bunks are built with stronger frames and higher limits. Check the rating on the listing or the label on the frame before you buy the mattress, because a mattress cannot fix a frame that is not rated for the sleeper. Then the depth rule still applies on the top bunk — 15cm, whatever the sleeper weighs — because the guard rail does not care how old the sleeper is. A heavier adult on a slim 15cm mattress will feel the slats through it more than a child will, so a pocket-sprung 15cm mattress, which spreads weight better than foam at the same depth, is usually the better choice for an adult top bunk. How often should you replace a bunk bed mattress?

Every six to eight years, sooner if the sleeper has outgrown it or the mattress has started to dip. Children's mattresses take more jumping than adult ones, and a dipped mattress on a top bunk is a safety issue as well as a comfort one — the sleeper sits higher, closer to the top of the guard rail.

Six to eight years is the usual lifespan for a bunk bed mattress, and it is shorter than an adult mattress for a simple reason: children jump on beds. Every bounce compresses the foam or the springs a little more, and over years the mattress dips in the middle, which is the sign it is done. A dip matters more on a top bunk than on a normal bed, because a dipped mattress lets the sleeper sit lower in the middle but higher at the edges — closer to the top of the guard rail — and because a mattress that has lost its support lets a growing child sleep in a curve night after night. Replace sooner if the child has outgrown the mattress — a teenager on a child's mattress is a teenager sleeping on a bed that no longer supports them — or if the mattress has stains or smells that will not wash out, because a bunk mattress is hard to air properly at the top of a frame. And when you do replace it, measure the bunk again: bunks get handed down, frames get swapped, and the depth rule has not changed since you last bought one.

Bunk bed mattress FAQs

Can I use a normal single mattress on a bunk bed?

Only if it is no deeper than 15cm on the top bunk. A normal single is the right size — 90 by 190cm — but most standard singles are 20 to 25cm deep, which leaves too little guard rail above the sleeper. Buy a mattress made for bunks, or measure your mattress and check it against the bed's maximum-mattress mark.

Is 15cm the maximum mattress depth for a bunk bed?

For most bunks, yes — Dreams and Silentnight both state 15cm as the maximum for the top bunk. A few bunks allow 16cm, and the Simba Hybrid Kids mattress is made at 16cm for those. The number that matters is the one marked on your bunk, not a general figure.

What is the best bunk bed mattress for a hot sleeper?

A pocket sprung mattress. The springs leave air gaps inside the mattress, so heat escapes instead of building up around the sleeper. Memory foam sleeps warmer, especially on a top bunk where warm air rises — if your child wakes up sweaty, springs are the fix.

Are bunk bed mattresses safe for children under 6?

The mattress is not the issue — the top bunk is. UK safety guidance says the top bunk is only for children aged 6 and over, whatever the mattress. Younger children sleep on the bottom bunk, where a standard-depth mattress is fine.

Do bunk bed mattresses come with a trial period?

It helps, especially for children who cannot describe what feels wrong. Several bunk mattresses come with a sleep trial — the Simba Hybrid Kids is one — so the child can sleep on it for a few weeks and you can send it back if it does not suit them.
