Best TV Beds UK (2026): Are They Worth It?

A TV bed sounds like the future. You're lying in bed, you press a button on a remote, and your television glides up out of the footboard like something from a film. No bracket on the wall, no screen staring at you all day, no argument about where the TV goes in a small bedroom.

That part is real — it does exactly that. The part nobody advertises is the motor doing the lifting. TV beds are a brilliant idea wrapped around a mechanical weak point, and this guide is the honest version of that conversation: how the mechanism works, what size TV actually fits, and the reliability questions most retailers hope you never ask.

What is a TV bed and how does it work?

A TV bed is a bed frame with a motorised lift hidden in the foot end that raises your television from inside the footboard at the press of a button. You operate it by remote control or a foot-end button, and the TV lowers back down flush when you're done — nothing to see but the bed.

Inside the footboard sits a metal lift column with an electric motor. Your TV bolts onto a bracket at the top of that column — the same kind of VESA mount pattern you'd find on a wall bracket — and the motor drives the whole thing up or down in about 20–30 seconds. Most models give you a small handheld remote; some add a button on the foot end itself, and a few advertise voice activation. Remotes get lost down the side of the mattress, and voice control on budget frames can be temperamental — test the control properly in the showroom before you buy.

Power and cables live inside the frame too. The lift needs a mains socket nearby — usually routed through the headboard or a cable port in the foot end — and your HDMI and aerial leads thread through the same cavity. Here's the fine print: on some frames the back of the TV presses close against the housing when the screen is fully raised, and the HDMI sockets end up blocked or awkward to reach. If you ever plug in a games console or a soundbar, check you can get to those ports with the screen up before you buy, not after.

Are TV beds worth the money?

Only if you'll use the feature properly. A TV bed typically costs £700–£1,200 mid-range and £1,200+ for premium models, roughly double a standard bed frame — so you're paying hundreds for the lift alone. If you watch TV in bed most nights, it's a genuine luxury; if it's a novelty that wears off, it's an expensive bed with a motor waiting to break.

Do the maths honestly. A solid non-TV bed frame in the same style might cost £300–£500, so the motorised lift is adding £400–£700+ to the bill. It suits one buyer best: you watch TV in bed most nights, you can't or won't wall-mount, and you'd genuinely pay extra for the screen to vanish when you're not watching. For that person, a TV bed transforms the bedroom.

The people it doesn't suit are easy to describe too. If this is a spare-room bed, or you only watch TV in bed when you're ill, you're paying a hefty premium for a feature that will sit unused while a motor and electronics age quietly underneath. Unused machinery doesn't stay young — and a failed motor turns your £1,000 purchase into a very expensive standard frame.

The cost-per-year reality check

Divide a £900 TV bed over a realistic 7-year life and you're at roughly £130 a year — against about £60 a year for a standard £450 frame. That £70-a-year difference is the price of the magic. But if the motor fails in year three and the repair bill runs into the hundreds, that number gets ugly fast — which is why the warranty questions below matter more than any headboard fabric.

What size TV fits in a TV bed?

Most UK TV beds take 32–43 inch televisions, and premium models fit up to 50–55 inch. Never go by screen size alone: check the VESA mounting pattern on the back of your TV and the lift's weight limit. A 55 inch TV that's too heavy or has an awkward VESA layout simply won't mount.

VESA is the bit buyers skip, and it's the bit that ruins purchases. It's the standardised pattern of screw holes on the back of your TV — numbers like 200×200 or 400×400 — and the lift bracket has to match it. The lift mechanism also has a maximum weight rating: a 55 inch screen can weigh 15–18 kg before you add a soundbar, so if your TV is older and chunky, check the scales before you order the bed.

One more measurement people forget: depth. Some older or cheaper TVs are thick-bodied, and the footboard cavity is a fixed size. If the TV is too deep, it won't sit flush when lowered, and the footboard won't close properly. Check the cavity dimensions in the product specification against your TV's depth. A quick browse of current TV bed listings on Amazon UK will show you how widely the max screen size varies between models — don't assume one frame's limit applies to the next.

And plan the room, not just the bed. TV beds are bulky pieces of furniture, and the foot-end housing adds length beyond a normal frame. Run your measurements through our bed size finder before ordering — a bed that won't fit through the door is a problem no motor can fix.

Should you get an ottoman TV bed or a non-ottoman one?

Get an ottoman TV bed if you need the storage — the whole mattress platform lifts on gas struts to reveal a cavernous compartment under the bed, which is gold in a box-room or a flat with no loft. If you already have storage sorted, a non-ottoman TV bed is lighter, cheaper and simpler, with fewer moving parts to fail.

This is really a question about your home, not about TVs. Ottoman storage swallows duvets, suitcases, winter coats and all the clutter that makes a small bedroom feel smaller. But it's a second mechanism — a gas-strut platform — bolted onto the same frame as the TV lift, and it adds weight, cost and complexity. Here's the honest side-by-side:

What should you check before buying a TV bed?

Three things: the motor warranty (length and who actually repairs it), your TV's VESA pattern and weight versus the lift's limits, and the returns policy. Owner reports include motors dying within days, TVs rising on their own at night, flaky remotes — and HDMI sockets blocked when the screen is up. Check all of it before you pay.

This is the section retailers can't afford to write, so we'll write it plainly. A TV bed is furniture with a machine inside it, and the machine is the part that breaks. Here's what to pin down:

  • Motor warranty length — and who repairs it. Ask outright: how long is the lift mechanism covered, and when it fails, who comes to fix it? A one-year warranty from a retailer with no repair network is a promise, not a plan. Owner reports of motors dying within days or weeks are not rare enough to ignore, and a dead lift turns your £1,000 bed into a very expensive standard frame with a TV-shaped hole in it.
  • TVs rising on their own at night. It sounds like a ghost story, but multiple owners report screens lifting themselves in the small hours — usually a faulty remote sensor or control box. Ask whether the control unit can be fully switched off or isolated at the mains when you're not using it. If the answer is a shrug, buy elsewhere.
  • Remote reliability. The remote is your only key to the feature you're paying for. Test it properly — range, responsiveness, whether the buttons work first time. And find out what a replacement costs and how long it takes to arrive, because remotes have a habit of vanishing between the mattress and the wall.
  • Noise level. Most lifts make a low mechanical hum as the screen rises — fine at 9pm, less fine at 1am if you're a light sleeper. Ask the retailer specifically. "Whisper quiet" on a product page is marketing; an actual decibel figure, or a demo video with sound, is evidence.
  • Access to connections with the screen raised. HDMI sockets, USB ports and aerial leads can end up blocked when the screen is fully up. If you run a console, a streaming stick or a soundbar, confirm you can reach the ports without dismantling the footboard.
  • Assembly. TV beds are heavy, and the lift mechanism is a serious piece of engineering, not flat-pack shelving. Check whether the retailer offers an assembly service and what it costs — and whether DIY assembly voids the motor warranty, which on some models it does.
  • Returns policy. This is a large, heavy piece of furniture. If it arrives faulty — or the motor dies in week two — how does the return work? Who pays for collection, and are there restocking fees? Get it in writing before the delivery van arrives.

The question that sorts good retailers from the rest

Ask this exact sentence: "If the motor fails in eighteen months, who repairs it and what does it cost me?" A good retailer has a crisp answer — a named repair partner, a warranty extension option, a parts price list. A bad one changes the subject back to the headboard fabric. You've just learned everything you need to know.

TV bed vs wall-mounted TV: which is better?

A wall-mounted TV costs £30–£80 for a bracket and a free weekend, versus hundreds extra for a TV bed — so on pure money, the bracket wins every time. A TV bed earns its keep only when you want the screen completely hidden for a clean bedroom look, or you can't wall-mount in a rental.

Let's be blunt about the comparison, because most buyers are choosing between these two and nothing else. A wall bracket is cheap, silent, has no motor to fail, and puts the screen exactly at eye level from the pillow. Its downsides are modest: you need a solid wall, a drill and a landlord who says yes — and the TV stares at you all day, which plenty of people genuinely dislike in a bedroom.

The TV bed's whole case rests on the disappearing act and the rental-friendly install. Just go in with your eyes open: you're trading the bracket's simplicity and near-zero running cost for a motorised mechanism that needs power and will one day need repairing. For some buyers that's a fair trade. For most others, the £50 bracket is the smarter buy.

TV bed FAQs

Can I put any TV in a TV bed?

No. Check the VESA mounting pattern on the back of your TV and the lift's maximum weight before buying — most UK TV beds suit 32–43 inch screens, with premium models taking up to 50–55 inch. A TV with an unusual VESA layout or one that's too heavy for the motor simply won't mount.

How long do TV bed motors last?

That is the honest unknown — the lift motor is the part most likely to fail, and owner reports include motors dying within days or weeks, screens rising on their own at night, and unresponsive remotes. Buy on the length of the motor warranty and who actually repairs it, not on the headboard stitching.

Are TV beds noisy?

Most lifts make a low mechanical hum as the screen rises, which is fine at waking hours but noticeable at midnight. Ask the retailer specifically about noise before buying, and be honest with yourself if you're a light sleeper — a wall-mounted TV is silent by comparison.

Do ottoman TV beds cost more than standard TV beds?

Typically yes. You're paying for two mechanisms instead of one — the motorised TV lift and the gas-strut storage platform. Only pay the premium if you actually need the storage; otherwise a non-ottoman TV bed is lighter, cheaper and has one fewer thing to break.

Will a TV bed fit in a small bedroom?

It can, but measure twice. The foot-end housing that holds the TV adds bulk beyond a normal bed frame, and king-size TV beds are substantial. Use our bed size finder to check the fit, and read our small bedroom storage bed guide if space is the whole point of the purchase.
