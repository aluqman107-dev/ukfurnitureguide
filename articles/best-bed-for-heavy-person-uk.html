Best Bed for a Heavy Person (UK 2026): Frames, Mattresses and Bases That Hold Up

Updated for 2026 · Independent advice — nobody pays for a place in this guide.

In short: if you weigh over 16 stone, buy the bed as a system, not a mattress on its own. You need a frame with a published weight rating that covers you, a firm or medium-firm hybrid mattress at least 25cm deep, and a base rated to match. A mattress rated to 20 stone on a frame rated to 15 is a 15-stone bed — the weakest part sets the limit.

Most bed shopping advice is written for someone around 11 to 13 stone. If you weigh considerably more than that, the usual guidance quietly stops working: a mattress that reviewers call "firm" feels medium under you, a frame that never creaks for a lighter sleeper starts talking at 2am, and a divan base that looks solid can sag in the middle within a year. None of that means you need a specialist medical bed. It means you need to shop by weight ratings instead of adjectives, and to check the frame and the base as carefully as the mattress.

A bed for a heavy person is a bed — frame, mattress and base together — where each part carries a published weight rating that covers the sleeper (or both sleepers, combined), and where the construction is built to hold that weight night after night without the middle dipping or the joints loosening. The rating is the thing to look for. "Heavy duty" in a product name means nothing; a number in stone or kilograms, printed by the manufacturer, means everything.

What weight rating should a bed have?

For a single sleeper, the mattress and the frame should each be rated to at least your weight, with some headroom — ideally 2 to 3 stone above it. For a couple, add both weights together and check the combined rating, because a "20 stone per sleeper" mattress on a frame rated to 30 stone combined is still a 30-stone bed for the two of you.

The ratings themselves are not standardised, which is the first thing to know. One brand's "rated to 20 stone" might be a tested engineering figure; another's might be a cautious estimate. Treat the number as a starting point, then look at what backs it up: the spring gauge, the foam density, the slat spacing, the centre support. A rating with construction details behind it is worth far more than a rating on its own. And check whether the rating is per sleeper or combined — the two get mixed up constantly in listings, and the difference matters. A mattress rated to 46 stone combined sounds enormous until you realise it means 23 stone each, which is a different proposition for a couple who are both over 18 stone. Where a listing doesn't state a rating at all, take that as information too: brands that test for weight tend to say so, because it's a selling point. If you can't find a number, email and ask before ordering — and if the answer is vague, move on.

REM-Fit rates its hybrid mattresses to 20 stone per sleeper, its bed frames to a combined 40 stone and its divan bases to 50 stone. Which bed frame is best for a heavy person?

A solid wooden frame or a well-built metal frame with a centre support rail and a centre leg — and slats spaced no more than about 7.5cm apart. The frame matters more than most people think, because it is the part that fails first: joints loosen, slats crack, and the middle sags long before the mattress gives up.

Start with the slats. They are the quiet heroes of a heavy sleeper's bed, and the usual failure point. Wide gaps between slats — 10cm or more — let the mattress dip between them, which is how you end up with a lumpy surface and a mattress guarantee voided at the same time. Most mattress guarantees require slats no more than about 7.5cm apart, so check the spacing before you buy and measure it when the bed arrives. Solid slats beat sprung slats at higher weights: sprung slats are designed to flex, which is lovely for a lighter sleeper and unhelpful when the flex never comes back. A centre support rail running down the middle of the frame, with its own leg to the floor, is the single most important feature on any double or king frame for a heavier sleeper — without it, the middle of the bed is held up by nothing but the side rails, and it will sag. Look for bolted joints rather than push-fit ones, and for corner brackets rather than bare screws into end grain. Our metal vs wooden bed frame comparison goes through the material trade-offs in detail, but the short version is this: a heavy, well-jointed wooden frame and a thick-gauge steel frame both work; a light, flat-pack frame with thin slats and no centre rail doesn't, whatever it's made of. Upholstered frames are fine too, provided the wooden frame underneath is properly built — the fabric tells you nothing about the structure, so ask what's inside it. And if the frame squeaks already, that's a warning, not a quirk: our guide to stopping a bed frame squeaking covers the fixes, but a frame that squeaks under a heavy sleeper from week one is a frame that was never rated for the weight.

The spring-count trap

More springs is not better for a heavier sleeper — it can be the opposite. Above about 2,000 springs in a king, manufacturers raise the count by using thinner-gauge wire in smaller units, which gains contouring and loses individual spring strength. What a heavier body needs is fewer, stronger springs with more depth: a spring count around 1,500 to 2,500 in a king, thicker gauge wire, and a mattress 25cm or deeper, so the lower layers — the ones a lighter person never reaches — are doing real work.

What mattress is best for a heavy person?

A hybrid — pocket springs plus a foam or latex comfort layer — in firm or medium-firm, at least 25cm deep, with a published weight rating. All-foam mattresses can work, but only with high-density foam, and they tend to sleep hotter and sag sooner under sustained weight.

Here's why the hybrid wins for most heavier sleepers. A pocket-spring core gives you pushback: each spring responds on its own, so the mattress supports your weight rather than just absorbing it. Foam on top provides the pressure relief at shoulders and hips. An all-memory-foam mattress, by contrast, relies entirely on foam density to hold you up, and under a heavier sleeper the foam compresses through to the base layers — which is exactly how the "sleeping in a dip" feeling starts. If you do go all-foam, look for high-density foam (manufacturers who publish density figures are the ones worth trusting) and expect a shorter lifespan. Firmness needs recalibrating too: a mattress rated 7 out of 10 is calibrated, loosely, for someone of average weight. Over about 15 stone, add roughly a point — a 7 will feel like a 6 under you. That means most heavier sleepers should be shopping in the 7–9 range, firm to very firm, and side sleepers should look for a firm mattress with a generous comfort layer rather than a medium one. Our firmness guide explains the scale in full, including the weight adjustment table. Depth is the other number that matters: 25cm is a sensible minimum, 30cm or more is better, because a heavier sleeper reaches the lower layers of the mattress that a lighter person never touches — those layers need to exist and need to be built from something substantial. Latex is worth a look as well: natural latex is responsive, sleeps cooler than memory foam and holds its shape well under weight, though it costs more. And whichever type you choose, the trial period is your safety net — use at least 30 nights of it, because a mattress that feels fine for ten minutes in a showroom can feel very different after a month.

Does the base matter as much as the mattress?

Yes — arguably more, because a good mattress on a weak base is a weak bed. A solid platform-top divan, a solid slatted base, or a closely-spaced slat frame all work. A sprung divan base or widely-spaced slats quietly undo the mattress you just paid for.

The base decides how firm the mattress feels and how long it lasts. A sprung divan base gives under weight, which makes the mattress feel softer than it did in the shop and lets it sag in the middle over time. A solid platform top doesn't give at all, so the mattress behaves as designed — firmer, flatter, longer-lived. If you're buying a divan, ask for the platform-top version and check its rating: REM-Fit's divan bases, for instance, are rated to a combined 50 stone, which tells you the manufacturer has actually thought about it. With slatted bases, the same 7.5cm spacing rule applies, and the slats themselves should be solid beech or birch rather than thin pine. One more base detail people miss: the gap between the mattress and the frame. If the mattress sits inside a frame with a lip, the edge support comes partly from the frame — fine. If it sits on top of a divan with nothing holding the edges, a mattress with weak edge support will feel like it's tipping you out of bed. Edge support matters more for heavier sleepers, because more weight sits nearer the edge when you sit on it to get up. What are the signs your bed isn't coping?

A dip in the middle that you can feel when you lie down, creaking that gets louder over weeks, rolling towards your partner in the night, and edges that collapse when you sit on them. Any one of these is a warning; two together mean the bed is failing.

The dip is the classic one, and the easiest to check: lay a straight edge — a broom handle works — across the mattress and look for the gap underneath. A dip of more than about 3–4cm in the sleeping area is a mattress that has given up, and a topper won't fix it, because the topper just follows the dip down. Creaking is usually the frame: joints working loose under load, or slats flexing against the rails. Tighten everything, check the slats for cracks, and if the creak comes back within weeks, the frame is under-rated for the weight — see the squeaking guide linked above for the full fix list. Rolling together is the couples' version of the dip: two people, one hollow in the middle, and it usually means the centre support is missing or the mattress core has compressed. Edge collapse — the feeling that you're going to slide off when you sit on the side to put your shoes on — is a mattress edge-support failure, and it's the one most likely to cause a fall. If your bed shows these signs and it's still within its guarantee period, photograph the dip with a straight edge and a tape measure and contact the retailer: most guarantees cover sagging beyond a stated depth, usually around 2.5–4cm. If it's out of guarantee, the honest answer is that patching it — new slats, a plywood board under the mattress, a topper — buys months, not years. The board trick in particular is worth knowing as a stopgap, but it traps moisture under the mattress and isn't a long-term fix.

What should couples with different weights do?

Buy a mattress rated for the heavier partner, then soften one side with a topper if the lighter partner needs it. Compromising on a mattress that's too soft for one of you is the mistake — too firm can be fixed, too soft can't.

This is the hardest version of the problem, and it's common: one partner at 12 stone, the other at 20, sharing one mattress. The rule is simple — the mattress has to support the heavier person, because a mattress that bottoms out under one sleeper ruins the night for both of them. Start with a firm or medium-firm hybrid rated for the heavier partner's weight, then add a soft topper on the lighter partner's side only. It sounds improvised, but it genuinely works: a 5cm soft topper changes the feel of one side by a step or two without touching the other. The other options are zip-and-link singles — two single mattresses zipped together, each with its own firmness — or a split-tension mattress, where each half is built to a different firmness. Both cost more, but they solve the problem properly rather than papering over it. And check the frame's combined rating, not just the per-sleeper one: two people at 18 stone each need a frame rated to at least 36 stone combined, with a centre rail and leg, or the middle will sag no matter how good the mattress is.

Best bed for a heavy person FAQs

What is the best bed for a heavy person?

A firm or medium-firm hybrid mattress at least 25cm deep with a published weight rating, on a frame with a matching rating, closely spaced solid slats and a centre support rail. The frame, mattress and base all need to be rated — the weakest part sets the limit for the whole bed.

What weight can a standard UK bed frame hold?

It varies widely, and many standard frames publish no rating at all. As a rough guide, a well-built double frame with a centre rail typically handles a combined 30–40 stone, but you should always check the manufacturer's stated rating for the specific model — if there isn't one, ask before buying.

Is a firm mattress better for a heavy person?

Generally yes — most heavier sleepers need firm or medium-firm (around 7–9 on the 1–10 scale), because a mattress rated for average weight feels about a point softer under a heavier body. Side sleepers should look for a firm mattress with a thick comfort layer rather than a medium one.

Can a heavy person sleep on a memory foam mattress?

Yes, but only a high-density one, and hybrids usually work better. Standard memory foam compresses through to the base layers under heavier weight and can sleep hot and sag sooner. A hybrid with pocket springs gives pushback that foam alone can't, and a latex layer is a good alternative.

How do I know if my bed is sagging?

Lay a straight edge such as a broom handle across the mattress and measure the gap underneath — a dip of more than about 3–4cm in the sleeping area means the mattress has failed. A topper won't fix a sag; it just follows the dip down. Photograph it with a tape measure if you're claiming under guarantee.
