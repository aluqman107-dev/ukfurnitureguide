Chaise Sofa vs Corner Sofa (UK 2026): Which Shape Fits Your Room?

Updated for 2026 · Independent advice — nobody pays for a place in this guide.

In short: a chaise sofa stretches one end out for lying down and needs one full wall; a corner sofa turns 90 degrees and needs two full walls. The chaise wins in small and awkward rooms — it reaches 60 to 90cm less along the second wall. The corner wins when three or more people need proper upright seats at once.

They look similar in a showroom. Both are big, both are L-shaped if you squint, and both get called a "corner sofa" by at least one retailer in every town. But they are different pieces of furniture that solve different problems, and buying the wrong one is a mistake you live with for years — a corner sofa in a room with only one long wall dominates the space and blocks the walkway, while a chaise in a big family room leaves someone without a proper seat every evening. The confusion is made worse by the names: UK shops use "corner", "L-shape", "chaise" and "L-shaped corner" almost interchangeably, and there is no fixed rule for what any of them means.

A chaise sofa is a straight sofa with one end extended into a long, deep seat you can stretch your legs out on; a corner sofa turns 90 degrees, with a full second run of seats along the next wall. That is the whole difference, and everything else — the floor space, the seating, the price, the room it suits — follows from it. A chaise pushes out into the room. A corner runs along a second wall instead. Once you see that, the choice gets much easier.

What is the difference between a chaise sofa and a corner sofa?

A chaise sofa extends one end into a long, deep seat for stretching out; a corner sofa turns 90 degrees, with a full second run of seats along the next wall. The chaise gives you one lounging spot and saves wall space; the corner gives you more upright seats and takes two full walls.

The easiest way to tell them apart is to count the sides and look at how each one ends. A corner sofa has two seating sides of roughly equal length meeting at a right angle, with a backrest behind you on both sides — it is built for sitting upright, in company, facing into the room. A chaise sofa has one main run and one end that extends toward you, with no backrest on the extended part — it is built for one person to lie down. That difference in the second side is the whole story: the corner's second side is a seat, and the chaise's second side is a leg rest. It is also why the two shapes need different rooms. A corner sofa needs two clear walls of similar length, because both sides are full seats that need a backrest against something. A chaise needs one full wall and a short stretch of the second, because the chaise only reaches partway along it. And it is why they feel different to live with: a corner sofa is a social piece, built for a household sitting together, while a chaise is a lounging piece, built for one person stretched out with a book. Neither is better in the abstract. They are better for different rooms and different households, and the room decides.

What is a chaise on a sofa?

A chaise is an extended seat built into one end of a sofa, deep enough to support your legs from hip to heel without a footstool. It is part of the sofa's frame, not a separate piece — that is what sets it apart from a freestanding chaise longue, which stands on its own.

The word comes from the chaise longue, the long chair that has been in British homes for centuries, and the idea is the same: a seat long enough to lie on. On a modern sofa the chaise is built into the frame at one end, so the sofa's outline becomes an L — but only at one end, and only in depth. A standard seat is about 55 to 70cm deep; a chaise seat runs 140cm or more, long enough for an adult to stretch out fully. That is the number that matters when you are comparing listings, because some retailers call any slightly deeper end seat a chaise, and a 90cm "chaise" is really just a wide seat with ambitions. The honest test is whether you can lie on it with your legs supported from hip to heel. If your feet hang off the end, it isn't a chaise — it is a deep seat, and you are paying chaise money for it. The other thing to know is that the chaise is what most people mean by a "corner chaise sofa": a sofa with a chaise at one end is often listed as an L-shape, and some shops sell it as a corner sofa outright. The name on the listing is no guide — the footprint is.

Corner, L-shape and chaise — the names are not standard

There is no fixed rule for these names in UK retail. Some shops say a corner sofa and an L-shaped sofa are the same thing, while others use "L-shape" to mean a sofa with a chaise and "corner" to mean two equal sides. Always check the footprint and the diagram in the listing, not the name — and measure both walls before you order.

Which is better for a small living room?

The chaise, almost always. It reaches 60 to 90cm less along the second wall than a corner sofa's second side, so it fits rooms where a corner would swallow the walkway — though a compact corner can work in a square room of 4 by 3.5 metres or more.

The arithmetic is straightforward. A corner sofa needs two walls, and both of them need to be long — a typical corner runs 235 by 235cm, so it takes over 2.3 metres of wall in both directions, plus the walkway in front of it. In a room under 4 by 3.5 metres, that leaves nowhere to walk and nowhere to put anything else. A chaise sofa uses one full wall and only part of the second: the chaise reaches 60 to 90cm less along the second wall than a corner sofa's second side, which is often exactly the difference between a walkway and a wall of furniture. That makes the chaise the right answer for the rooms Britain actually has — the long thin terrace lounge, the flat where the living room is also the dining room, the room with a door or a radiator breaking up the second wall. The corner's one small-room case is the square room: if the room is roughly square, at least 4 by 3.5 metres, and both walls are clear, a compact corner can work, and it seats more people than a chaise in the same space. How much floor space does each one need?

A typical corner sofa runs 235 by 235cm up to 260 by 260cm, needing two full walls; an L-shaped chaise sofa runs roughly 212 by 164cm to 290 by 160cm, using one full wall and a short stretch of the second. Measure both walls, not just the long one.

Those footprints come from a Yorkshire maker that publishes the measurements for the same ranges in both shapes, and they are a useful reality check, because they show how much of the room each shape actually takes. The corner is the bigger commitment in both directions: 235 by 235cm at the small end, 260 by 260cm at the large, and it needs those walls clear — no radiator, no door swing, no side table in the way. The chaise is longer on its main run — up to 290cm — but much shorter on the second side, 160 to 164cm, which is the side that usually causes the problem. The practical way to decide is to mark it out on the floor with masking tape before you order: tape the footprint of each shape, live with it for a day, and walk the route you actually walk — from the door to the kitchen, from the sofa to the window. The tape doesn't lie, and it is much cheaper than a delivery that doesn't fit. Leave 75cm of walkway around the sofa, and 45 to 60cm between the sofa front and the coffee table, and if the tape eats either of those, the shape is too big for the room. What does left-hand and right-hand mean on a corner sofa?

Stand facing the front of the sofa: if the long side runs to your left, it is a left-hand corner; to your right, a right-hand corner. The catch is that some sellers name it from the seated position instead, which flips the label — so always check the diagram in the listing, not just the name.

This is the single most common ordering mistake with corner and chaise sofas, and it is entirely avoidable. Most UK sellers name a corner sofa by the side its longer section, or chaise, sits on as you stand facing the front of it. Long side on your left is a left-hand corner sofa; long side on your right is a right-hand one. But some sellers describe it from the seated position instead — sitting on the sofa, looking out — which flips the name completely, so the same sofa is left-hand on one site and right-hand on another. The fix takes ten seconds: look at the drawing in the listing rather than trusting the label, and match it to your room. Stand where you will look at the sofa from, usually the doorway or the television, and point to the wall the long side will run along. If the listing offers a reversible chaise, or a modular set you can rearrange, the choice matters less — but most fixed corner and chaise sofas are handed, and a wrong-handed sofa is a return, a restocking fee and a week without a sofa. If the listing has no diagram at all, don't order it: a retailer that won't show you which side is which is a retailer that doesn't want the return either.

Which is better for lying down and watching television?

The chaise wins for one person lying flat — its long seat supports your whole body without a footstool. The corner wins for a household: two or three people can sit upright with a backrest behind them, which is what an evening of television with the family actually needs.

This is really a question about how your household watches television, and it is worth answering honestly rather than aspirationally. If the evening is one person, stretched out, laptop or book in hand, the chaise is the better seat — it is a bed with a backrest, and nothing else in the living room does that job. If the evening is two or three people, the corner is the better seat, because it gives everyone a backrest and a proper upright position, and nobody ends up perched on the arm or on the floor. The corner's other advantage is that it is the shape that faces the television properly from two directions at once, which matters in a room where the television sits in the corner or on the long wall. The chaise's weakness as a family seat is the second person: the chaise end has no backrest, so the second person sits upright on the main run while the first person lies down, and the room's seating is effectively one-and-a-half. The corner's weakness as a lounging seat is the opposite: nobody can lie flat on it without their feet in someone's lap. Some corner sofas answer this with a recliner section or a chaise end built into the corner — our best recliner sofa guide covers the recliner versions — but a plain corner is a sitting sofa, not a lying one, and it is worth knowing which you actually need before you choose.

Does a chaise sofa cost more than a corner sofa?

Usually less. A chaise is one straight run with an extended end, so it uses less frame, less fabric and fewer sections than a corner sofa, which needs two full runs and a corner piece. The gap narrows on premium ranges, where the fabric and the build matter more than the shape.

The price difference is real but smaller than people expect, and it is driven by materials rather than by marketing. A corner sofa has more of everything: more frame, more foam, more fabric, and a corner section that has to be built to join two runs at a right angle without sagging or creaking. It also usually arrives in two or more sections that clip or bolt together, which adds to the build cost and the delivery cost. A chaise is simpler — one run, one extended end, often delivered in a single piece or two — so it costs less to make and less to move. But the shape is only one factor in the price. A premium chaise in a good velvet will cost more than a budget corner in a thin polyester, and the fabric, the frame guarantee and the cushion filling matter more than the shape on any like-for-like comparison. As with every sofa sold in the UK, both shapes must meet the Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations 1988 and carry a permanent label — check for it on delivery, whatever the shape. And if the choice is genuinely close, buy on the room, not the price: the cheaper shape that doesn't fit is the most expensive sofa you will ever buy.

Chaise sofa vs corner sofa FAQs

What is the difference between a chaise sofa and a corner sofa?

A chaise sofa extends one end into a long, deep seat for stretching out; a corner sofa turns 90 degrees with a full second run of seats along the next wall. The chaise saves wall space and suits one person lying down; the corner seats more people upright and needs two full walls.

Is a chaise sofa the same as an L-shaped sofa?

Almost — a chaise sofa is the most common type of L-shaped sofa, but the terms are used loosely in UK retail. Some shops call any L-shaped sofa a corner sofa, and some use "L-shape" only for chaise designs. Check the footprint and the diagram, not the name.

Do corner sofas need two walls?

Yes, effectively. A corner sofa needs two clear walls of similar length, because both sides are full seats with backrests. If your second wall is short, broken by a door or a radiator, a chaise sofa is the better fit — it only needs a short stretch of the second wall.

Which is better for a small living room, a chaise or a corner sofa?

The chaise, in most small rooms. It reaches 60 to 90cm less along the second wall than a corner sofa, so it leaves the walkway clear. A compact corner only works in a square room of at least 4 by 3.5 metres with both walls clear.

Can you move the chaise to the other side?

Only on some designs. Most fixed chaise sofas are handed — left or right — and cannot be swapped. Reversible-chaise and modular designs let you move it, so if you might rearrange the room or move house, look for one of those before you buy.
