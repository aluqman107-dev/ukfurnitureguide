Best Low Profile Beds (UK 2026): What Counts as Low, and Who Should Buy One?

Updated for 2026 · Independent advice — nobody pays for a place in this guide.

In short: a low profile bed sits 20 to 40cm off the floor at the slats, so the sleeping surface lands around 40 to 55cm high once the mattress is on. It makes small and low-ceilinged rooms feel bigger, suits children and loft conversions, and costs less than a standard frame — but it is a poor choice if your knees complain or you need the space underneath for storage.

Walk into a bedroom with a low bed and the room feels different before you have worked out why. The ceiling seems higher. The window seems bigger. There is more floor visible, more air above the mattress, and the whole room reads as calmer and less crowded — even though nothing in it has moved except the bed, which has dropped by about twenty centimetres. That is the whole trick of a low profile bed, and it is a real one: in a small British bedroom, where the bed is usually the biggest thing in the room, lowering it is the cheapest way to make the room feel larger.

A low profile bed is a bed frame whose sleeping surface sits close to the floor — usually a platform frame with short legs or no legs at all, and no box spring underneath. The frame itself is typically 20 to 40cm high, so with a standard mattress on top the sleeping surface lands around 40 to 55cm off the floor, noticeably lower than a standard UK divan. Low beds come in every style — plain pine platform frames, upholstered frames with a low headboard, Japanese-inspired floor beds — but the idea is the same in all of them: take the height out of the bed and give it back to the room. This guide covers what counts as low, who it suits, who should avoid it, and the frames worth buying in the UK.

What is a low profile bed?

A low profile bed is a bed frame that sits close to the floor, with a low slat height and no box spring, so the mattress rests 20 to 40cm off the ground. Most are platform-style frames in wood or metal, and the low height takes up less visual space and makes the ceiling feel higher.

The phrase gets used loosely, so it helps to pin it down. A low profile bed is not a mattress on the floor, and it is not a divan with short legs. It is a frame designed to be low: a platform base — slats or a solid panel — held a short distance off the floor by short legs, a plinth, or nothing at all. There is no box spring, no deep base, and no tall headboard doing the visual work. The mattress sits directly on the slats, which is why the sleeping surface ends up so much lower than a standard bed, where the mattress sits on a base that already stands 30cm or more off the floor. The style most people picture is the platform bed: a simple wooden or upholstered rectangle, often with a low headboard or none, that looks like it grew out of the floor rather than standing on it. Japanese-inspired floor beds are the extreme end of the same idea, with side rails as low as 9cm. At the other end, upholstered low frames with a padded headboard give you the look without giving up the headboard entirely — the frame is low, but the bed still reads as a bed rather than a platform.

How low is a low profile bed?

Frame height is the number that matters, and low profile frames sit 20 to 40cm off the floor at the slats. Add a standard 20cm mattress and the sleeping surface lands around 40 to 55cm high — roughly knee height or just below it — while a standard divan with a mattress usually sits 60cm or more off the floor.

Retailers rarely publish one clean number, so here are the figures that are actually printed on UK listings. The Zinus Maya, one of the best-selling low upholstered platform frames, stands 30cm high at the frame — that is the whole frame, legs included — with a wooden slat base and no box spring needed. Floor beds go lower: the Nova Montessori low floor bed from Strictly Beds & Bunks has side rails just 9cm high, with the mattress sitting on a heavy-duty solid pine slatted base a few centimetres off the floor. A vidaXL floor bed frame in solid pine measures 200 by 200 by 10cm — ten centimetres of frame, with the mattress doing the rest of the height. And low sleeper beds, the category built for loft rooms, sit 20 to 40cm from the ground at the sleeping platform. The number to watch is not the frame height alone but the frame plus the mattress: a 30cm frame with a 25cm mattress puts you at 55cm, which is low but not dramatically so, while the same frame with a 15cm mattress puts you at 45cm, which feels properly low. Work out the total before you buy, because the mattress is half the height of the bed.

The measurement that matters

Always add the frame height and the mattress depth together. A "low" frame with a deep mattress is not a low bed at all — it is a standard-height bed wearing a low frame. For a sleeping surface around 40 to 50cm off the floor, pair a 20 to 30cm frame with a 15 to 20cm mattress.

Who should buy a low profile bed?

Anyone whose room feels cramped, low-ceilinged or over-furnished, and anyone who likes the look. Low beds suit loft rooms, box rooms and flats with low windows, they make small bedrooms feel bigger, and they suit children moving out of a toddler bed, because the fall from a low bed is a short one.

Small rooms are the clearest case. In a box room or a flat bedroom where the bed fills most of the floor, a low frame gives back the visual space a tall divan takes away: you see more floor around the bed and more wall above it, and the room reads as bigger even though the measurements have not changed. Loft conversions are the second case, and the most practical — with eaves as low as 120 to 160cm at their lowest point, a low sleeper frame is often the only bed that leaves enough headroom to sit up. Children are the third: a low bed is one of the safest choices for a young child, because the fall from the mattress is only a few centimetres, and Montessori-style floor beds are built exactly for that. And then there is the style case, which is not a small thing: low beds suit the modern, minimal bedrooms that fill Instagram and Pinterest — pale wood, white walls, no clutter — and if that is the room you want, a low frame is how you get it. They also suit anyone who sleeps hot in a small room, because there is less frame trapping warm air around the mattress, though that is a small effect next to the mattress itself.

Who should avoid a low profile bed?

Anyone who struggles to stand up from a low seat. If your knees complain getting out of a low sofa, a low bed will be worse, because you push up from lying flat. Older sleepers, people with bad backs or dodgy knees, and anyone who uses the space under the bed for storage should buy a standard-height frame instead.

This is the part the glossy photos leave out, and it matters more than the look. Getting out of a low bed is a squat: you push yourself up from lying flat, with your knees bent deep and your back doing the work, and if your knees or your back are already unhappy, that movement twice a day — getting up in the morning and getting into bed at night — becomes a daily annoyance. Older sleepers should think hardest about this, because a bed that is easy to get out of at fifty can become a problem at seventy. The second group who should skip low beds is anyone who stores things under the bed. A low frame has nothing underneath it — that is the point — so the suitcases, the spare duvet and the out-of-season clothes have to live somewhere else, and in a small flat that somewhere is usually the wardrobe, which is already full. The third group is anyone with a bad back who needs a firm edge to sit on while putting shoes on: a low bed has no edge to sit on at a useful height, and you end up dressing on the floor.

What mattress works best on a low profile bed?

A medium-depth mattress of 15 to 25cm, on a slatted base with gaps no wider than 7cm. Skip the box spring entirely — that is the point of a platform frame — and choose a mattress you can lift easily, because changing the sheets on a low bed means bending further than you are used to.

The mattress is half the bed, so it deserves the same attention as the frame. On a low profile frame the right mattress is a medium-depth one, 15 to 25cm: deep enough to be comfortable, shallow enough to keep the sleeping surface low. A deep 30cm mattress on a low frame defeats the purpose — you end up at standard height, having paid for a low bed. The base matters too: slats should sit no more than 7cm apart, or a soft mattress will sag between them, and a solid panel base needs ventilation holes or the mattress will trap moisture underneath. Foam mattresses suit low frames well because they are light — you will be lifting the mattress to change the sheets, and on a low bed that means bending right down, so every kilogram counts. Pocket-sprung mattresses are heavier and harder to manoeuvre at floor level, though they sleep cooler, which is worth weighing up if you run hot. And one practical note: buy a mattress with handles if you can. They are a small thing on a standard bed and a genuine help on a low one.

Do low profile beds work in loft rooms?

Yes — they are often the only beds that do. A loft conversion needs at least 75cm of headroom above the mattress for sitting up, and with eaves as low as 120 to 160cm at their lowest point, a low sleeper frame sitting 20 to 40cm off the floor is what makes the maths work.

Loft rooms are where low beds stop being a style choice and become a practical necessity. The rule, from bed retailers who fit lofts every day, is that you need at least 75cm of headroom above the mattress to sit up in bed without hitting the ceiling — and in a typical UK loft, where the lowest point of the eaves sits between 120 and 160cm, a standard bed eats that headroom before you have even added the mattress. A low sleeper frame sitting 20 to 40cm off the floor leaves the mattress at 40 to 55cm, which leaves 75cm or more above it in most lofts, and that is the difference between a room you can sleep in and a room you can only crawl into. Position matters as much as the frame: put the bed where the ceiling is highest, usually against the tall wall or under the ridge, and keep the ladder or the door clear of the low side. And check the exact model before you buy — some low sleepers are built for children and rated accordingly, so adults should check the weight limit and the slat strength, not just the height.

Should you choose a wooden or metal low profile bed?

Wood for warmth and quiet, metal for a slimmer frame and a lower price. Solid pine platform frames are the classic low bed — sturdy, silent and easy to repair — while metal frames are lighter, cheaper and easier to move, though cheap ones can creak where the joints flex.

The material decision on a low bed is the same as on any bed, but the low height makes the differences more noticeable. Wood — usually solid pine at the budget end, oak or ash higher up — is warm to look at, silent in use, and easy to fix: a loose joint takes a screw and some wood glue, and a scratched rail takes a dab of wax. Solid pine platform frames are the classic low bed for a reason, and they are also the easiest to find in the UK, from the Futon Company's low platform storage beds to the Montessori floor beds made in Bedfordshire. Metal frames are slimmer, lighter and cheaper, and they suit the industrial or minimalist look that low beds often go with — but cheap metal frames can creak where the joints flex, and a creak at floor level carries through a quiet house at night. Upholstered low frames are the third option: a padded frame in velvet or fabric, usually with a low headboard, that softens the look and gives you something to lean against. They cost more, they collect dust and pet hair, and they are harder to clean — but in a bedroom where the bed is the statement piece, they are often the best-looking choice. Is a low profile bed the same as a mattress on the floor?

No, and the difference matters. A mattress on the floor traps moisture underneath and has no airflow, which is how mould starts. A low profile frame lifts the mattress onto slats, so air circulates underneath, the mattress stays dry, and you still get the low look without the damp.

The floor-mattress look is all over social media, and it is tempting — it costs nothing, and it looks the same in a photo. But a mattress on the floor is a different thing from a low bed, and the difference shows up within months. A mattress needs air underneath it: your body gives off moisture through the night, and on a frame that moisture passes through the slats and away, while on the floor it has nowhere to go, so it collects between the mattress and the floorboards. That is how mould starts, and it starts on the underside where you cannot see it until the smell gives it away. A low profile frame solves this for the price of a few centimetres: the slats lift the mattress off the floor, air circulates underneath, and the mattress stays dry. The frame also keeps the mattress off the dust and the draughts at floor level, and it gives the bed an edge — a frame to tuck the sheets under, a rail to stop the mattress sliding. If you like the floor look, buy the lowest frame you can find rather than skipping the frame entirely. Your mattress will last years longer for it.

Low profile bed FAQs

How low should a bed be?

The frame should sit 20 to 40cm off the floor at the slats, giving a sleeping surface of roughly 40 to 55cm once the mattress is on. Anything lower starts to feel like a mattress on the floor, and anything above 55cm stops feeling low at all.

Are low profile beds good for your back?

The height makes no difference to your back — the mattress and the slats do. A low bed with a good medium-firm mattress supports you exactly like a standard-height bed. The height only matters for getting in and out, which is harder on a low frame if your knees or back are already unhappy.

Can you put a normal mattress on a low profile bed?

Yes, as long as it is not too deep. A 15 to 25cm mattress suits a low frame; a deep 30cm mattress on a low frame defeats the point and pushes the sleeping surface back up to standard height. Measure the frame plus the mattress together before you buy.

Do low profile beds have storage?

Almost never. The whole point of a low frame is that there is nothing underneath it, so you lose the under-bed space a divan or an ottoman gives you. If storage matters more than the look, a divan with drawers or an ottoman bed is the better buy.

Are low beds safe for children?

Yes — they are one of the safest choices for young children, because the fall from the mattress is only a few centimetres. Look for a solid pine frame tested to BS EN 1725, like the Montessori-style floor beds, and keep the mattress depth modest so the child can climb in and out alone.
