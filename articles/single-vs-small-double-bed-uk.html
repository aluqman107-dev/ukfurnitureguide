Single vs Small Double Bed (UK 2026): Is the Extra 30cm Worth It?

Updated for 2026 · Independent advice — nobody pays for a place in this guide.

In short: a small double is 120cm wide against a single's 90cm, and both are 190cm long. That extra 30cm is the cheapest comfort upgrade in the bedroom — worth it for any adult who sleeps there every night, if the room can take it. For two adults every night, though, step up to a standard double at 135cm.

It is the quietest decision in bed buying, and the one people get wrong most often. The single is the default — the bed everyone grew up in, the size every spare room seems to have. The small double is the size most people have never heard of until a retailer mentions it, usually while they are standing in a bedroom that is slightly too small for a double and slightly too big for a single. It sits in the gap between the two, and for a surprising number of British bedrooms, it is the right answer.

A small double bed — also called a 4ft bed or a three-quarter bed — is a UK bed size measuring 120cm wide by 190cm long, sitting between a single (90cm) and a standard double (135cm). It is the same length as both, so the whole decision is about 30cm of width: whether the room can spare it, and whether the sleeper will feel it. This guide is about answering both of those questions honestly, with the room maths, the two-person reality and the bedding catch that catches people out.

What is the difference between a single and a small double bed?

Width, and only width. A UK single is 90cm wide and a small double is 120cm wide, and both are 190cm long. That extra 30cm is the difference between a bed that fits one person snugly and one that gives a solo sleeper room to turn over — or squeezes two people in at a push.

Everything else about the two sizes is the same. Same length, same mattress depth options, same choice of frames — divan, wooden, metal, ottoman — and the same headboard styles. The difference is entirely in the width, and 30cm sounds small until you sleep in it. On a 90cm single, an adult sleeper has about the width of their own shoulders plus a few centimetres either side; turning over means a conscious movement, and the edge of the mattress is always close. On a 120cm small double, the same sleeper has room to turn, to stretch an arm out, to sleep diagonally on a bad night — the small freedoms that add up to better sleep. The names don't help. A small double is also sold as a 4ft bed, a three-quarter bed and, confusingly, sometimes as a "queen" — though in the UK "queen" isn't an official size and can mean different things depending on the retailer, so ignore the name and read the centimetres. The frame is always a little larger than the mattress, and upholstered, wingback and sleigh beds can be noticeably wider and longer because of the headboard and side rails, so check the external dimensions on the product page, not just the mattress size.

How much bigger is a small double than a single?

30cm wider and no longer: a single is 90 by 190cm, a small double is 120 by 190cm, and a standard double is 135 by 190cm. The small double sits exactly between the two — 30cm wider than a single, 15cm narrower than a double — which is why it is also called a three-quarter bed.

Thirty centimetres is worth picturing properly, because it is the width of a standard pillow, or a bedside table, or the gap between a bed and a wall that decides whether you can get out on that side. It is also a third more sleeping width than a single — 120cm against 90cm — which is a bigger jump than the number suggests. The other comparison that matters is the one with the double: a small double is only 15cm narrower than a standard double, and that 15cm is often the difference between a bed that fits the room and one that doesn't. In a room where a double leaves 45cm of walkway and a small double leaves 60cm, the small double is the bed that lets you get out of bed without turning sideways. And the length is the same across all three — 190cm, or 6ft 3in — so a small double suits sleepers up to about 5ft 10in comfortably, and anyone taller should look at the 200cm lengths that come with king sizes, or a European size, which runs 10cm longer but needs a matching European frame and mattress. Watch out for European sizes

Some beds, especially Scandinavian designs, are 10cm longer: a European small double is 120 by 200cm, not 120 by 190cm. A UK small double mattress won't fit a European frame properly, so always match the mattress to the size stated on the bed, not just the name — and check the length, not only the width.

Can two people sleep in a small double?

Yes, but snugly: a small double gives each person about 60cm of width, which is less than a single bed gives one person on their own. It works for couples who like sleeping close, for occasional guests, and for teenagers — but for two adults every night, a standard double at 135cm is the practical minimum.

Sixty centimetres per person is the number to hold onto, because it is the whole argument. A single bed gives one person 90cm. A small double shared by two gives each person 60cm — less than a solo single, and about the width of a standard pillow each, with nothing to spare. Couples who sleep close, who don't move much, and who like the warmth of it will manage fine, and many do for years. Couples who sprawl, who sleep hot, or where one person is a restless sleeper will find it a nightly negotiation, and the smaller of the two will usually end up with less than their half. The honest rule is the one the bed retailers use themselves: a small double is the smallest size that fits two adults at all, and a standard double at 135cm — 67cm each — is the practical minimum for most couples. For a guest room that occasionally sleeps two, the small double is a good compromise. For a teenager's room where a friend stays over sometimes, it is ideal. For a couple's main bedroom, if the room can take a double, buy the double. The 15cm between the two sizes costs less than the sleep it buys.

What size room do you need for a small double?

A room around 2.4 by 2.7 metres takes a small double comfortably, with a walking strip down one side and room for a bedside table. The absolute minimum is about 2.5 metres wide for access on both sides, or 1.9 metres if you accept access from one side only.

The room maths is three numbers: the bed, the walkway and the door. The bed takes 120cm of width, plus a few centimetres for the frame — upholstered frames add more, slim metal frames add almost nothing. The walkway needs 60cm on at least one side for comfortable access, and the same at the foot so you can get out without climbing over the end. Add those together and a room 2.4 metres wide takes a small double with a walking strip down one side and room for a bedside table, while a room 2.5 metres wide allows access on both sides. In a typical small bedroom of 2.5 by 3 metres, a small double leaves roughly 65cm of clearance at the sides and 75cm at the foot, while a standard double in the same room drops below 50cm and starts to feel tight. The length matters too: you need the 190cm mattress plus the headboard, which runs from 5cm on a slim panel to 15cm on a deeply padded one, plus a small allowance at the foot so the duvet doesn't press against the wall — around 2.1 metres of length is the practical minimum. And the number people forget is the door: a door that swings into the room and sweeps across the space where the bed would sit is the most common reason a plan fails, so check the swing arc on the floor before you order. If the room is genuinely tiny, a single is practical and sensible — but if it takes a small double, the extra 30cm is worth having. Is a small double big enough for a teenager?

Yes — it is arguably the best bed size for a teenager. A small double gives a growing teenager room to stretch out and space for a laptop, a book and a phone beside them, without taking over a bedroom that still has to fit a desk, a wardrobe and a door that opens.

The teenager's bedroom is where the small double earns its keep, because it is the room where every centimetre is doing two jobs. A single is enough for a child, but a teenager is nearly adult-sized, and a 90cm bed that was fine at twelve is a squeeze at sixteen — the feet hang over the end, the shoulders touch both edges, and the bed becomes somewhere to sleep rather than somewhere to live. A small double gives a growing teenager room to stretch out, space for a laptop and a book beside them, and enough width that a friend staying over isn't a crisis. It also fits the room a teenager actually has: a bedroom that still has to hold a desk, a wardrobe, and a door that opens, which a standard double often can't do without blocking one of them. The frame matters here too. A divan with drawers turns the space under the bed into storage for clothes and school kit, which is worth more in a teenager's room than in almost any other — our best divan beds under £300 guide covers the budget end of that choice. And if the teenager is tall, check the length: 190cm suits sleepers up to about 5ft 10in, and a tall teenager will want the extra length of a king-size frame or a European 200cm size.

Is bedding harder to find for a small double?

Slightly, yes. The range of frames, mattresses and accessories is narrower than for a standard double, and some high-street ranges skip the 4ft size for fitted sheets altogether. Check the bedding before you order the bed — a small double is 120 by 190cm, and a standard double sheet will not fit it properly.

This is the small double's real weakness, and it is worth knowing before you buy rather than discovering it in a shop. Small double mattresses are stocked by most major UK brands, but the range is narrower than for standard doubles — fewer models, fewer firmness options, fewer sale prices — because the size sells in smaller numbers. Bedding is the tighter squeeze. Fitted sheets, duvet covers and mattress protectors all come in 4ft, but not every high-street range carries them, and some retailers skip the size entirely for fitted sheets, so a standard double sheet — 135cm wide — will bunch and pull on a 120cm mattress. The fix is simple: buy the bedding at the same time as the bed, from a retailer that lists the 4ft size, and check that the fitted sheet, the duvet cover and the mattress protector are all available before the bed arrives. Online, the range is much better than on the high street, and the price difference is small. The mattress itself needs the same care: a small double mattress is 120 by 190cm, and a single or a double mattress will not fit the frame — a 30cm gap at the side is a safety problem, not a cosmetic one, because the mattress won't be supported properly. Single vs small double: which should you buy?

Buy the single if the room is tight, the sleeper is a child, or the bed is for occasional guests. Buy the small double if one adult sleeps there every night and the room can take it — the extra 30cm is the cheapest comfort upgrade in the bedroom, and it costs less than the next size up.

The decision is really two questions, and they have to be answered in order. First, can the room take it? Measure the room, allow 60cm of walkway on one side and at the foot, check the door swing, and if the small double fits with those clearances, it is a candidate. If it doesn't fit, the single is the answer, and it is a perfectly good one — a single is practical, sensible and absolutely fine for a child, a guest room or a box room used as an office with a spare bed. Second, who sleeps in it? A child, an occasional guest or a solo adult who sleeps still will be happy on a single. An adult who sleeps there every night, a teenager, or anyone who turns over in their sleep will feel the extra 30cm every single night, and it is the cheapest comfort upgrade in the bedroom — the step from a single to a small double costs far less than the step from a small double to a double, and it buys a third more sleeping width. The one case where neither is right is the couple: if two adults share the bed most nights, the standard double at 135cm is almost always worth the extra 15cm, for the wider range of frames, mattresses and accessories alone. Single vs small double bed FAQs

What size is a small double bed in the UK?

A standard UK small double measures 120cm wide by 190cm long — 4ft by 6ft 3in. It is 30cm wider than a single (90cm) and 15cm narrower than a standard double (135cm). The terms "small double", "4ft bed" and "three-quarter bed" all mean the same size.

Can two adults sleep in a small double bed?

Yes, but snugly — each person gets about 60cm of width, which is less than a single bed gives one person alone. It works for couples who sleep close and for occasional guests, but for two adults every night, a standard double at 135cm is the practical minimum.

Is a small double the same as a queen bed?

No — in the UK, "queen" isn't an official size and can mean different things depending on the retailer, from a small double to a king. A UK small double is always 120 by 190cm; a queen-size mattress in many European markets has different dimensions, so always check the centimetres.

What size room do you need for a small double bed?

Around 2.4 by 2.7 metres allows comfortable access on one side with room for a bedside table. The minimum workable width is about 2.5 metres for access on both sides, or 1.9 metres if you accept access from one side only — plus 60cm of clearance at the foot.

Do small double beds need special bedding?

Yes — a small double takes 120 by 190cm bedding, and a standard double sheet will not fit it properly. The range is narrower than for standard doubles, and some high-street ranges skip the 4ft size for fitted sheets, so check the bedding is available before you order the bed.
