How to Stop a Bed Frame Squeaking (UK 2026): Find the Noise, Fix It in an Evening

Updated for 2026 · Independent advice — nobody pays for a place in this guide.

In short: a squeak is two surfaces rubbing where they shouldn't — usually a loose bolt, a slat moving in its housing, or the frame rocking on an uneven floor. Find it first (frame or mattress, then which joint), tighten everything, cushion the contact points with felt or tape, and only then consider replacing anything. Most squeaks are fixed with a spanner, felt pads and twenty minutes.

There is a special kind of tiredness that only a squeaky bed produces. Every turn becomes a noise, every noise wakes the other person, and within a week you're both sleeping rigidly in the middle of the mattress like guests in your own bed. The good news is that bed squeaks are one of the most fixable problems in the house. The bad news is that spraying WD-40 at random, which is what most people try first, usually fixes nothing — because the squeak is rarely where you think it is.

A bed squeak is friction: two parts of the bed system moving against each other as weight shifts across the mattress. The parts are almost always joints, slats, the centre support, the floor contact, or — the twist — the mattress itself, which squeaks in a way that sounds exactly like the frame. Work through the diagnosis in order and you'll fix the right thing first time.

Why does my bed squeak when I move?

Because something in the bed system has loosened enough to move. Bolts work loose over months of nightly loading, wooden joints dry out and shrink slightly, slats shift in their housings, and a frame that was level on day one rocks on a floor that was never quite flat. Each tiny movement rubs two surfaces together, and that rubbing is the squeak you hear.

Age makes it worse, but new beds squeak too — often worse, because flat-pack frames settle in the first few weeks as the joints bed in. A squeak that appears a month after assembly is usually just the frame asking to be tightened once. The pattern of the noise tells you a lot: a squeak on every turn, from the same corner, points to one joint; a groan when you sit on the edge points to the side rail or the centre support; a noise that follows you wherever you press points to the slats or the mattress. Note when it happens before you take anything apart — it halves the search.

Is the squeak coming from the frame or the mattress?

Do this test before touching a single bolt: take the mattress off, put it on the floor, and lie on it, roll around and press down hard in several places. If it squeaks on the floor, the mattress is the culprit — usually a broken or tired spring in a pocket-sprung mattress — and no amount of frame work will fix it. If it's silent on the floor, the frame is guilty.

This five-minute test saves more wasted effort than any other step in this guide, because mattress squeaks and frame squeaks sound identical from above. If the mattress is the problem, flip or rotate it if it's designed to be flipped — some aren't, so check the label — and if the squeak persists, the mattress is telling you it's done. Pocket springs that have started to squeak rarely stop, and they usually mean the support is going with them. Our memory foam vs pocket sprung comparison is worth reading at that point, because a foam mattress has no springs to squeak, which is a genuine advantage for light sleepers. If the mattress is silent on the floor, put it back and hunt the frame: press down on each corner of the empty frame in turn, rock it gently, and listen. The corner or joint that answers is your culprit.

The floor test

Still not sure? With the mattress off, press down firmly on the middle of the frame, then on each side rail. A frame that rocks diagonally — one corner lifting as the opposite presses down — is sitting on an uneven floor, not a broken frame. That is a feet-and-levelling fix, not a replacement, and it is the most common cause of squeaks in older houses and flats with original floorboards.

How do you stop a wooden bed frame squeaking?

Tighten every bolt, then wax the joints. Wooden frames squeak where wood meets wood or wood meets metal fixings: the mortise-and-tenon joints dry out, the bolts holding the side rails work loose, and slats rattle in their slots. A socket wrench or Allen key through every fixing, followed by a rub of candle wax or a bar of soap on the joint surfaces, silences most wooden frames completely.

Work methodically, one joint at a time, and don't over-tighten — stripped threads in softwood are worse than a loose bolt, because they never hold again. If a bolt spins without gripping, remove it, wrap the thread with a couple of turns of PTFE tape on Amazon UK, and refit it; the tape takes up the slack and stops the rattle. Check the slats next: a slat that has shrunk slightly will sit loose in its housing and tap with every movement. A strip of felt, an old sock, or a piece of fabric wrapped around the slat end cushions it silently. And check for the quiet killer: a cracked slat. It flexes differently from its neighbours, throws the load unevenly, and squeaks in a way that sounds like a joint. Replace it — slats are cheap, standard sizes are widely available, and a frame with one new slat is a frame that stops complaining.

How do you stop a metal bed frame squeaking?

Tighten the bolts, cushion the tube joints, and lubricate only metal-on-metal contact. Metal frames squeak where tubes slot together, where bolts pass through brackets, and where the frame meets the floor — three different fixes, and using the wrong one on the wrong spot is why the "spray everything" approach fails.

Start with the bolts, as with wood. Then look at the tube joints: where one tube slides inside another, a wrap of PTFE tape or a strip of fabric around the inner tube stops the metal-on-metal chatter. For bracket joints, a felt pad or rubber washer between the two metal faces does the same job. Lubricant has its place — a silicone spray lubricant on Amazon UK or a few drops of oil on a metal hinge or pivot joint — but keep it off wood, off fabric, and off the floor, and wipe away the excess; a pool of oil under a bed attracts dust and stains carpet. One metal-frame habit worth breaking: don't use the frame as a lever to move the bed. Dragging a metal frame across the floor loosens exactly the joints you're trying to keep tight. Lift it, or use furniture sliders under the feet. How do you stop a divan bed squeaking?

Check the drawer runners and the base joints first, then the headboard fixing. Divan squeaks are sneaky because the base is upholstered — you can't see the joints — so the noise seems to come from everywhere. Nine times out of ten it's a drawer runner working loose, the two base halves shifting against each other, or the headboard bolts loosening where the headboard meets the base.

Empty the drawers and open them one at a time, pressing down on the base as you go. A drawer that squeaks when loaded but not when empty has a runner problem: tighten the runner screws, and add a smear of candle wax to the runner channel. If the base is two halves — common in king size and super king divans — check the linking clips underneath; if they've worked loose, the halves shift independently and the whole bed talks. Tighten or replace the clips, and make sure the base sits flat: divans on carpet can sink unevenly, which loads one half more than the other. The headboard is the other divan suspect: the bolts that fix it to the base loosen over time, and a headboard that moves a millimetre every time you sit up will squeak like a door hinge. Tighten them, and slip a felt pad between the headboard and the wall if the headboard itself is knocking the wall — which brings us to the next section.

How do you stop the headboard banging against the wall?

Put something soft between the headboard and the wall, and stop the bed moving. The bang is the headboard hitting plaster; the cause is the bed shifting a few millimetres with every movement. Felt pads or a strip of foam stuck to the back of the headboard silence the impact, but if the bed keeps wandering, the fix is underneath: felt or rubber pads under the feet grip the floor and stop the drift.

In rented flats this matters twice over, because a headboard knocking the wall marks the paint — and paint marks are deposit deductions. A row of thick felt pads across the back of the headboard costs very little and protects both your sleep and your deposit. If the bed still moves, check whether the floor is the problem: on hard floors, rubber feet grip far better than bare wood or plastic glides, and on carpet, a bed with no centre support leg can walk slowly across the room over months. Some people wedge the bed with a rolled towel behind the headboard; it works, but it looks terrible and the towel migrates. Felt pads are the tidy version of the same idea. If the headboard is a separate piece bolted to the frame, tighten those bolts as part of the same job — a loose headboard bangs harder than a tight one.

What if the floor is uneven?

Level the bed, don't rebuild it. An uneven floor makes a rigid frame rock, and a rocking frame squeaks at every diagonal corner in turn. Check with a spirit level across the frame, then pack the low feet: felt pads stacked to the right thickness, purpose-made furniture levellers, or a folded piece of hardboard under the foot. The goal is all feet — including the centre support leg — bearing weight evenly.

The centre support leg deserves its own check, because it's the most overlooked squeak source in the whole bed. On doubles and kings, the centre rail carries a large share of the load, and if its leg doesn't reach the floor properly — too short, sunk into carpet, or sitting in a dip in the floorboards — the rail flexes and the slats above it chatter. Press down in the middle of the bed: if the frame dips and the squeak answers, the centre leg is your problem. Extend it, pack it, or replace it with an adjustable leg, and the whole bed firms up. In older houses with springy floorboards, a frame that was silent in the shop and squeaks at home is almost always a floor problem, not a frame problem — which is reassuring, because floors are cheaper to accommodate than frames are to replace.

When is a squeaky bed beyond fixing?

When the structure itself is damaged: a cracked side rail, a split headboard joint, fixings that spin freely in stripped holes, or a frame that wobbles after every bolt is tight and every foot is level. Those are not maintenance issues — they are the frame telling you its working life is over. A mattress with a broken spring is the same story on the mattress side.

Use this as the decision rule: if you have done the floor test, tightened everything, cushioned the joints and levelled the feet, and the bed still squeaks loudly enough to wake you, stop spending evenings on it. A new frame is cheaper than months of bad sleep, and a frame bought with silence in mind — solid wood, screwed slats, a proper centre support — will stay quiet for years. Squeaky bed FAQs

Why does my bed squeak every time I turn over?

Because a joint, slat or fixing has loosened enough to move as your weight shifts. The most common causes are loose bolts, a slat moving in its housing, or the frame rocking on an uneven floor. Tighten everything first — that alone fixes most squeaks.

How can I tell if the squeak is the mattress or the frame?

Take the mattress off and lie on it on the floor. If it squeaks on the floor, the mattress is at fault — usually a tired or broken spring. If it is silent on the floor, the frame is guilty, and you should press each corner of the empty frame to find the noisy joint.

Does WD-40 stop a bed squeaking?

Sometimes, on metal-on-metal joints — but it is not a cure-all. It does nothing for loose bolts, loose slats or an uneven floor, and it should be kept off wood, fabric and carpet. Tighten and cushion the joints first, and use lubricant only where metal rubs on metal.

How do I stop my bed frame moving on a wooden floor?

Fit rubber or felt pads under every foot, including the centre support leg, and make sure all feet bear weight evenly. Rubber grips hard floors far better than bare wood or plastic glides, and it also stops the frame scratching the floor — which matters in a rented flat.

When should I replace a squeaky bed frame?

When the structure is damaged — a cracked rail, a split joint, fixings that spin in stripped holes — or when the squeak survives tightening, cushioning and levelling. At that point the frame's working life is over, and a new solid frame with screwed slats and a proper centre support will stay quiet for years.
