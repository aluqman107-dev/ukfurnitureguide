What Mattress Firmness Do I Need? (UK 2026): The Scale, Decoded

Updated for 2026 · Independent advice — nobody pays for a place in this guide.

In short: most UK sleepers are best served by a medium to medium-firm mattress — around 5–7 on the 1–10 scale. Side sleepers go softer, front sleepers firmer, and heavier sleepers firmer than lighter ones on the same mattress. The scale is a rough guide, not a standard: a "7" from one brand can feel like a "6" from another, so use the trial period, not the label, as the final test.

Mattress firmness is the most confusing number in furniture shopping. Every brand uses the same 1-to-10 scale, no two brands calibrate it the same way, and the number describes how the mattress feels under a standard weight that may have nothing to do with yours. No wonder people buy a "medium-firm" mattress, find it feels like a plank or a hammock, and conclude that mattress shopping is guesswork. It isn't guesswork — it just needs translating.

Mattress firmness is how hard or soft the surface feels when you lie on it, usually rated from 1 (very soft) to 10 (very firm), and it is different from support, which is whether the mattress keeps your spine in a sensible position all night. A mattress can feel firm and still support badly, and a soft-feeling mattress can support well — the two words are not interchangeable, and confusing them is how most firmness mistakes happen.

How does the UK mattress firmness scale work?

The 1–10 scale runs from very soft (1–2) through soft (3–4), medium (5), medium-firm (6–7) and firm (8) to very firm (9–10). Most mattresses sold in the UK sit between 4 and 8, and the majority of bestsellers cluster at 6–7, medium-firm. Crucially, there is no legal standard or industry body behind the numbers — each manufacturer sets its own.

That lack of a standard is the whole problem, and the reason you should treat the scale as a starting point rather than a specification. A 6 from a bed-in-a-box brand built around foam can feel noticeably softer than a 6 from a traditional pocket-sprung maker, because foam and springs compress differently under the same weight. Some brands also use their own words instead of numbers — "gentle", "supportive", "orthopaedic" — which tell you even less. "Orthopaedic" in particular means nothing legally: any mattress can be called orthopaedic, and it usually just signals "firm". Ignore the word, look at the construction: spring count, foam density and the depth of the comfort layers are what actually decide how a mattress feels. The other thing the scale hides is that firmness is weight-dependent. The numbers are calibrated, loosely, for someone around 11–13 stone. If you weigh much less or much more than that, the same mattress is effectively a different firmness under you — which is the next section.

What firmness do side sleepers need?

Medium to medium-soft — around 4–6 on the scale. Side sleepers need the mattress to give at the shoulder and hip, the two bony points that take the pressure, while still supporting the waist. Too firm, and those points ache; too soft, and the waist sags and the spine curves sideways all night.

Side sleeping is the most common position in the UK, and it is the position where firmness matters most, because the contact area is small and the pressure is concentrated. A mattress with a decent comfort layer — 5cm or more of foam or a pillow-top — lets the shoulder sink in while the spring unit underneath holds the rest of you level. That combination, a soft top over a supportive core, is what most side sleepers actually mean when they say they want a "soft" mattress: they want the top to yield, not the whole mattress to sag. If you wake with a dead arm or a sore hip, your mattress is probably too firm for side sleeping, whatever the label says. If you wake feeling like you've slept in a dip, it's too soft. What firmness do back and front sleepers need?

Back sleepers need medium-firm, around 6–7; front sleepers need firm, around 7–8. Both positions need the hips held up rather than allowed to sink — the difference is that front sleepers need more of it, because lying face down with sinking hips arches the lower back backwards all night.

For back sleepers, the test is the hand under the lower back: lying on the mattress, you should be able to slide a hand under your lumbar curve with slight resistance — not freely, and not not at all. Too much gap means the mattress is too firm and isn't meeting your back; no gap at all means it's too soft and your hips have sunk past the point of support. Front sleepers have the least room for error of any position, and a soft mattress is genuinely unhelpful for them — the stomach sinks, the spine extends, and morning stiffness follows. A firm mattress with a thin comfort layer is the honest recommendation, and front sleepers who also like a plush feel are better served by a firm mattress plus a thin topper than by a soft mattress. If you switch positions during the night — most people do — medium-firm is the compromise that offends nobody, which is exactly why it sells best. Combination sleepers should also favour responsiveness: foam that you sink into makes turning over hard work, while a spring or responsive-foam surface lets you move without waking yourself up.

Does body weight change the firmness I need?

Yes, more than any other single factor. The same mattress feels softer under a heavier person and firmer under a lighter one, because firmness is really about how far you compress the materials. As a rough rule: under about 9 stone, go one step softer than the position guide suggests; over about 16 stone, go one step firmer.

This is the adjustment most shoppers miss, and it explains most "the reviews loved it but I hate it" experiences. A lighter sleeper on a firm mattress barely engages the comfort layers at all — they lie on top of the mattress rather than in it, the shoulder and hip pressure never gets relieved, and the mattress feels like a board. A heavier sleeper on a soft mattress compresses straight through the comfort layers onto the support core, bottoms out, and gets neither comfort nor support. Weight also decides durability: heavier sleepers compress foam more deeply every night, so foam density matters — look for high-resilience or high-density foams in the comfort layers, and a spring unit with a higher spring count and thicker gauge wire, rather than chasing a firmness number. Couples with a big weight difference face the hardest version of this: one mattress, two different firmness needs. The practical answers are a medium-firm compromise, a zip-and-link pair of singles with different firmnesses, or a split-tension mattress if the budget allows. A topper on one side only is a cheaper fix that genuinely works — it softens one sleeper's side without touching the other's.

Is firmness the same as support?

No — and confusing the two causes more bad mattress purchases than anything else in this guide. Firmness is how the surface feels in the first few seconds. Support is whether your spine stays in a sensible, neutral position for eight hours. A cheap firm mattress can feel hard while supporting badly, because its springs are too few or its foam too thin to hold you level.

Think of it this way: firmness is the handshake, support is the job. A mattress with a high spring count and good zoned support — firmer under the hips, softer under the shoulders — supports well whether it feels plush or firm on top. A mattress that is simply hard, with a thin comfort layer over a basic spring unit, feels "supportive" for a week and then reveals itself as a board with no give where you need it. The practical way to judge support is time: lie on the mattress for at least ten minutes in your normal sleeping position, in a shop if you can, and notice where you feel pressure and where you feel held. At home, the trial period is the real test — most UK bed-in-a-box brands offer 100 nights or more, and you should use at least 30 of them, because your body needs time to adjust from the old mattress. The "firm is better for your back" myth

It is one of the most persistent pieces of mattress folklore, and it is wrong. Very firm mattresses are not better for backs in general — the evidence and the experience both point to medium-firm as the sweet spot for most people, because it supports the spine while still cushioning the pressure points. A board-like mattress forces the shoulders and hips to take all the pressure, which is why people wake stiff on mattresses that were sold to them as "good for your back". If back discomfort persists, a clinician — not a mattress salesman — is the right next step.

What if I buy the wrong firmness?

Don't panic — firmness is the most adjustable mattress attribute there is. Too firm is fixed with a topper: a 5cm memory foam or latex topper softens the surface by a step or two without replacing the mattress. Too soft is harder, because nothing on top adds support — but a firmer topper, a solid base instead of a sprung one, or flipping a double-sided mattress can all firm things up a little.

A topper is genuinely the best £50–£150 you can spend on a firmness mistake, and it works in both directions if you choose the material honestly: soft foam softens, dense latex firms up the feel slightly while adding responsiveness. What a topper cannot do is rescue a mattress that has sagged — a dip in the middle is structural, and a topper just follows the dip down. That distinction is the subject of our mattress topper vs new mattress guide, which tells you when a topper is money well spent and when it is money thrown after a dead mattress. The other fix people forget is the base: a mattress on a sprung divan base feels softer than the same mattress on a solid slatted base, because the base gives too. If your new mattress feels softer than it did in the shop, check what it's sitting on before you blame the mattress. And if you are within the trial period, use it — returning a mattress is free with most online brands, and a month of testing beats a decade of guessing.

Mattress firmness FAQs

What is the best mattress firmness for most people?

Medium to medium-firm — around 5–7 on the 1–10 scale. It is the range where spinal support and pressure relief overlap for most positions and weights. Side sleepers should lean softer within that range, front sleepers and heavier sleepers firmer.

Is a firm mattress better for your back?

Not necessarily. Very firm mattresses are not better for backs in general — medium-firm is the sweet spot for most people, because it supports the spine while cushioning the shoulders and hips. A board-like mattress concentrates pressure on bony points and can leave you stiffer in the morning.

How do I know if my mattress is too firm?

You will feel pressure at the shoulders or hips, wake with a dead arm, or find a gap under your lower back when lying on your back. A 5cm soft topper usually fixes a too-firm mattress by a step or two — a much cheaper test than replacing the whole mattress.

What firmness should couples with different weights choose?

Start with medium-firm as the compromise, and adjust one side with a topper if needed. Other options are zip-and-link singles with different firmnesses, or a split-tension mattress. The key is that the heavier partner needs a firmer surface to avoid sinking through the comfort layers.

Does the 1–10 firmness scale mean the same thing across brands?

No. There is no legal standard behind the scale, so each manufacturer sets its own numbers — a 7 from one brand can feel like a 6 from another. Use the scale to build a shortlist, then rely on the trial period and on construction details like spring count and foam density to decide.
