Divan Bed vs Ottoman Bed (UK 2026): Storage, Access and the Honest Verdict

Updated for 2026 · Independent advice — nobody pays for a place in this guide.

In short: an ottoman bed stores far more — the whole base lifts to reveal one big compartment — but you have to clear the bed to open it. A divan with drawers stores less, but the drawers open with the bed made and get used every week. Buy the ottoman for bulky seasonal storage; buy the divan for things you reach for daily.

They look like the same bed. Same upholstered base, same mattress on top, same headboard at the end. Walk into a showroom and the two sit side by side, and the difference between them is invisible until someone shows you how each one opens — and then it is obvious, and it changes everything about which one you should buy. The divan opens at the sides, one drawer at a time. The ottoman opens from the top, the whole mattress lifting on gas struts like the boot of a car. Both are storage beds. They store different things, in different ways, for different people.

A divan bed is a solid upholstered base with pull-out drawers built into its sides; an ottoman bed is a base that lifts on gas struts to reveal the whole space underneath as one big storage well. That is the whole difference, and it decides the storage volume, the access, the price, the lifespan and the room each one needs. This guide compares them on all five, with the real numbers, so you can choose on the facts rather than the showroom pitch.

What is the difference between a divan bed and an ottoman bed?

How you reach the storage. A divan bed has a solid upholstered base with pull-out drawers in the sides; an ottoman bed has a base that lifts on gas struts, like a car boot, to reveal the whole space underneath as one big compartment. Same idea, completely different way in.

The divan is the older design, and the simpler one. It is a mattress on a solid, upholstered base that sits close to the floor on small feet or castors, and the base gives the mattress a firm, even foundation, so the whole bed feels supportive and stable. Storage versions have two or four drawers built into the sides — or sometimes at the foot end — and the drawers pull out on runners like any chest of drawers. Divans are typically sold as a base, with a headboard and mattress added separately or as a set, and they come with or without storage: a plain divan with no drawers is the cheapest upholstered bed you can buy. The ottoman looks like a divan until it opens. The whole top of the base is hinged, usually at the headboard end, and supported by gas-lift pistons — raise the platform with the mattress still on it, and the full footprint of the bed becomes a storage well. Ottoman bases usually open in one of two ways: end-lift, hinged at the headboard so the base rises from the foot, or side-lift, hinged along one side. The gas struts take most of the weight, so lifting the base is easier than it looks — but the base has to be clear to open, which is the catch that decides more purchases than any other factor in this guide.

How much storage does each one actually hold?

Far more than the drawers suggest, and far less than the ottoman promises. A king ottoman's well measures 750 to 1,050 litres, against 120 to 200 litres across four divan drawers — but usable figures run lower, around 270 litres for a king ottoman against 130 litres for a divan, once the frame and fittings take their share.

The numbers look contradictory until you know what each one is measuring. The big ottoman figure — 750 to 1,050 litres for a king, at 25 to 35cm deep — is the raw volume of the well: the whole footprint of the bed, 150 by 200cm, multiplied by its depth. It is a real number, and it is also a number nobody ever achieves, because the frame, the pistons, the mattress support and the lid mechanism all take space inside the well, and because a well that deep is hard to fill to the top without crushing what is at the bottom. The figures retailers quote as usable are much smaller: an extra-deep double ottoman offers around 210 litres against about 100 litres across four divan drawers, a king ottoman around 270 litres against 130 litres, and a small double ottoman around 175 litres against 80 litres. That is still roughly double the divan at every size — the ottoman's advantage is real, and it is about 100 to 120 percent at every size — but it is double a small number, not the tenfold gap the raw figures suggest. The divan's figure is easier to trust, because a drawer is a box: four drawers at 30 to 50 litres each is 120 to 200 litres for a king, and what you see is what you get. The practical difference is what each one holds. An ottoman swallows the bulky, awkward things: spare duvets, suitcases, a folded travel cot, winter coats, the Christmas decorations, sports kit. A divan holds the flat, everyday things: spare bedding, towels, out-of-season clothes folded flat, shoes. If the problem is suitcases and boxes, no drawer divan will solve it. If the problem is spare bedding, drawers solve it better, because you can reach it without stripping the bed.

The honest storage test

Before you buy, list what you actually need to store, not what you might store one day. If the list is suitcases, duvets and boxes, you need an ottoman. If the list is spare bedding, towels and clothes you reach for weekly, you need a divan. Storage you have to clear the bed to reach gets opened twice a year; storage you can open with the bed made gets used.

Which is easier to use every day?

The divan, by a distance. Drawers pull open with the bed made, the lamp still on and a cup of tea on the bedside table; an ottoman needs the bed cleared completely before it lifts, so it gets opened twice a year while the drawers get used every week.

This is the difference that matters most in real life, and it is the one the showroom hides, because in a showroom the bed is always made up beautifully and nobody has to move anything to open it. In a real bedroom, the bed is where things accumulate: the laptop, the book, the laundry, the phone charger, the clothes for tomorrow. To open an ottoman you have to clear all of it, lift the mattress platform — which is heavier than it looks, even with the struts helping — get what you need, and put it all back. It takes two minutes and a clear floor, and most people stop doing it within a month. The divan's drawers open with the bed made and the lamp still on, which means they get used weekly rather than seasonally — for the spare duvet, the clean towels, the clothes you reach for every day. There is a second, quieter difference: what happens when the mechanism fails. A divan drawer that sticks can be fixed with a drop of wax on the runner. An ottoman whose struts have weakened is a two-person job with a part to order. And there is a third: the weight on the mattress. An ottoman's gas lift is rated to lift a mattress of 100 to 150kg, which is enough for a regular mattress — but a heavy mattress, a topper and a thick duvet together can push past it, and then the lid gets heavier every year until it won't stay up on its own.

Which is better for a small bedroom?

The ottoman, if storage is the priority. It needs no floor space beside the bed to open — the lid lifts straight up — while a divan drawer needs 55 to 60cm of clear floor beside the bed to pull fully out. In a room where the bed touches the wall, the ottoman wins on the maths alone.

Small bedrooms are where the ottoman's design finally makes sense, and it is worth doing the maths properly. The government's nationally described space standard sets a minimum of 11.5 square metres for a double bedroom and 7.5 square metres for a single in new homes in England, with the main double bedroom at least 2.75 metres wide — and many British bedrooms, especially in terraces and flats, are smaller than that. In a room that tight, a divan drawer is a problem: it needs 55 to 60cm of clear space beside the bed to pull fully out, and a bed pushed against a wall on one side can only use the drawers on the open side, so there is no sense paying for four when two are reachable. An ottoman needs no floor space at all to open — the lid lifts upwards, so the bed can sit against the wall on three sides and still open fully. That is a genuine advantage, and it is why ottomans are the better fit for most small bedrooms, provided the storage is for bulky, seasonal things rather than everyday ones. The divan's small-room case is the room with space on one side: if you can leave 60cm clear beside the bed, the drawers work, and they work better than an ottoman for daily use. The other small-room factor is the frame itself: both designs give the mattress a firm, supportive base, so the sleep is the same — the difference is entirely in the storage, and the storage that fits your room is the storage you will actually use. What breaks first on an ottoman bed?

The gas struts. They are rated to lift a mattress of 100 to 150kg, and they do the heavy work every time the base opens — over years they weaken, the lid gets heavier, and eventually it won't stay up. A divan has no moving parts to fail, which is why it usually outlasts the ottoman it sits next to.

Every ottoman has a weak point, and it is the same on all of them: the gas-lift struts. They are the part that does the work, and they are the part that wears out. A strut is a sealed cylinder of compressed gas, and over years of opening and closing — and of holding a heavy mattress and topper up while you rummage underneath — the seal weakens, the pressure drops, and the lid that used to rise with one hand starts needing two, then starts drifting down on its own. Replacing them is possible — struts are a standard part, and a pair costs far less than a new bed — but it is a job, and it is a job that needs the mattress off and the base empty. The other ottoman failure is the hinge: a base that has been opened with weight on one side, or slammed shut, can twist the hinge out of true, and then the lid doesn't sit flush. A divan has none of this. Its drawers run on runners, its base is a solid box, and there is nothing to wear out except the drawer runners themselves, which are a five-minute fix with a screwdriver. That is why divans usually outlast ottomans: not because they are built better, but because there is less in them to break. It is also why the guarantee matters more on an ottoman — check whether the struts are covered, and for how long, before you buy. If you move house often, the divan wins again: it is lighter, it often splits into two halves, and it goes up the stairs in pieces. An ottoman is heavier, and it usually has to be emptied and partly dismantled before it moves at all.

Which costs more — a divan or an ottoman bed?

The ottoman, usually by a clear margin. A double ottoman starts at roughly £400 to £600 in the UK, with king sizes at £600 to £900, while a divan with drawers in the same size is typically cheaper — the gas-lift mechanism and the heavier frame are what you are paying for.

The price gap is the mechanism, and it is worth knowing what you are paying for. An ottoman costs more because it has more in it: the gas struts, the hinge, the reinforced base that has to hold a mattress and a full load of storage without sagging, and the heavier frame that carries all of it. A double ottoman starts at roughly £400 to £600, king sizes typically run £600 to £900, and super king ottomans can reach £1,000 to £1,500 for quality builds. A divan with drawers in the same size is typically cheaper, and a plain divan with no storage is cheaper still — our best divan beds under £300 guide covers the budget end, where a divan with a mattress included is still achievable. The question is whether the extra money buys anything you need. If the answer is storage volume — if the alternative is renting a storage unit, or living with suitcases in the hallway — then the ottoman pays for itself quickly. The Self Storage Association UK notes that around three-quarters of self-storage use comes from household customers, with not having enough room at home given as the main reason, which is the problem both of these beds exist to solve. If the answer is just a place for the spare bedding, the divan is the cheaper bed and the better one, because the drawers will actually get used. And if the budget is tight, remember that both designs are sold as base-only or as a set with a mattress and headboard, and the set price is where the real comparison belongs — a cheap ottoman with a thin mattress is a worse bed than a good divan with a decent one.

Divan vs ottoman: which should you buy?

Buy the ottoman if you need maximum storage for bulky, seasonal things and your room has no space beside the bed. Buy the divan if you reach for your stored things weekly, you move house often, or you want the bed that will still be working in ten years with nothing to fix.

The verdict is a use-case verdict, not a winner, because the two beds solve different problems and the right one depends on what you store and how often you reach for it. Buy the ottoman if your wardrobes are full, you live in a flat without a loft, and the things you need to store are bulky and seasonal — spare duvets, suitcases, winter clothes, the Christmas decorations. It turns the whole area under your mattress into storage, it needs no floor space beside the bed, and it is the most space-efficient bed you can buy. Buy the divan with drawers if you want quick, everyday access to a few items without lifting anything — the spare bedding, the towels, the clothes you reach for weekly — and if you move house often, or you want the bed that will still be working in ten years with nothing to fix. There is one more case worth naming: the spare room. If the bed is for guests, the divan is usually the better call, because the drawers hold the spare bedding and open with the bed made, while an ottoman in a room used as an office or a drying room between visits is a bed you have to clear before you can open. Divan bed vs ottoman bed FAQs

Which has more storage, a divan or an ottoman bed?

The ottoman, by roughly double. Usable figures run around 270 litres for a king ottoman against 130 litres across four divan drawers, and the raw well volume of a king ottoman is 750 to 1,050 litres. But the ottoman's advantage is for bulky, seasonal things — for everyday items, the divan's drawers are more useful.

Can you open an ottoman bed with the mattress on?

Yes — the gas struts are rated to lift a mattress of 100 to 150kg, so the base lifts with the mattress still on it. But the bed itself must be clear: you have to move the pillows, duvet, laptop and everything else off the bed before it will open.

Do ottoman beds break easily?

The frame is sturdy, but the gas struts weaken over years and eventually the lid won't stay up on its own. Struts are a standard replaceable part, and the guarantee should cover them — check the length of cover before you buy. A divan has no moving parts to fail.

Can a divan bed go against a wall?

Yes, but only the drawers on the open side will work — a drawer needs 55 to 60cm of clear floor beside the bed to pull fully out. If the bed touches the wall on one side, there is no sense paying for four drawers when two are reachable.

Is an ottoman bed worth the extra money?

Yes, if you need maximum storage for bulky, seasonal things and your room has no space beside the bed — the ottoman is the most space-efficient bed you can buy. If you only need somewhere for spare bedding you reach for weekly, a divan with drawers is cheaper and gets used more.
