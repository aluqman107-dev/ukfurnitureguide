Trundle Bed vs Day Bed (UK 2026): Which One Fits Your Spare Room?

Updated for 2026 · Independent advice — nobody pays for a place in this guide.

In short: a day bed is a single bed with a back and sides that works as a sofa by day and a proper bed at night; a trundle bed is a second bed on wheels that hides underneath and rolls out when guests stay. Buy the day bed if the room needs seating, add the trundle if guests stay often — and remember the trundle needs about 190cm of clear floor beside the bed to pull out.

The spare room is the hardest room in the house to furnish, because it has two jobs and they fight each other. By day it is an office, a playroom, a reading corner, or just the room with the ironing board in it. By night, a few times a year, it has to sleep one or two people properly — not on a camp bed, not on an air mattress that deflates at 3am, but on a real bed. The day bed and the trundle bed are the two pieces of furniture built for exactly this problem, and they solve it in different ways: one by being a bed that looks like a sofa, the other by being a bed that disappears entirely until it is needed.

A day bed is a single bed with a back and sides that doubles as a sofa by day; a trundle bed is a second, low bed on wheels that slides out from underneath another bed. That is the whole difference, and it decides the floor space, the comfort, the price, and which one your room actually needs. This guide compares them on all four, with the real measurements, so you can choose on the facts rather than the showroom photo.

What is the difference between a trundle bed and a day bed?

A day bed is a single bed with a back and sides, dressed with cushions so it works as a sofa during the day. A trundle bed is a second bed on castors that hides underneath another bed and rolls out at night. One is a bed that looks like seating; the other is a spare bed that disappears.

The confusion comes from the names, because the two are often sold together and the listings blur them. A day bed is a piece of furniture in its own right: a single bed frame, usually taking a standard UK single mattress at 90 by 190cm, with a back panel and two side panels, so that pushed against a wall and dressed with cushions it reads as a sofa. By day you sit on it; by night you take the cushions off and sleep on it. Nothing folds, nothing unfolds, and the mattress is a proper full-depth one of your choice. A trundle bed is not a piece of furniture in its own right at all — it is a second bed that lives underneath another bed. It sits on a low wheeled base, usually with its own slatted frame, and when someone stays over you pull it out on its castors and it becomes a second sleeping surface at floor level. When nobody is staying, it slides back underneath and the room looks like it has one bed in it. The key point is that a trundle is an add-on, not an alternative: it can sit under a day bed or under an ordinary single bed, which is why so many listings sell the two together as a day bed with a trundle.

Can a day bed have a trundle?

Yes — and the best guest-room setups do exactly that. Many UK day beds are sold with a trundle underneath, so one piece of furniture seats one person by day and sleeps two at night. The combination is so common that most listings call it a day bed with trundle, rather than two separate products.

This is the combination that makes the day bed work as a guest bed rather than just a single sleeper. Land of Beds sells its Penny blue velvet day bed with a pull-out trundle on castors that takes a single mattress up to 17cm deep, and its Stanley metal guest bed with a trundle that lifts to main-bed height on extendable legs, so the two singles sit side by side as a double-width sleeping surface. Time4Sleep's day beds with guest underbeds work the same way, with prices from around £269. The trundle in these sets is a proper second bed, not a camp bed: it has its own slatted base, it takes a standard single mattress, and it rolls out on castors in seconds. The catch is the mattress depth — the trundle mattress has to be slim enough to slide back underneath, and the limit varies by model, from 13cm on some wooden frames to 17cm on the Penny and 20cm on the Stanley's lifting trundle. Check the number on the listing before you buy the mattresses, because a mattress that is too deep will not slide back under the bed, and you will find that out on the first night, not in the shop.

How much floor space does a day bed need?

A standard UK day bed takes a 90 by 190cm single mattress, and the frame runs about 201 to 203cm long and 101 to 105cm deep once the back and side panels are counted. Allow a little over a metre of floor depth before cushions, and roughly two metres of wall to sit it against.

The frame is bigger than the mattress, and that is the measurement that catches people out. The back panel and the two side panels add roughly 10cm to the length and 10 to 15cm to the depth, so a day bed that sleeps one person takes up about the same floor as a small double bed — around two metres of wall and a little over a metre of floor depth. That is still a good deal for a spare room, because the same footprint gives you seating by day: dressed with cushions along the back, the day bed is a sofa that happens to be a bed, and the room keeps its daytime use. The height is the other number worth knowing: the seat sits around 76 to 83cm to the top of the frame, with the mattress a few centimetres below the rail, which is higher than a typical sofa seat and firmer too — a day bed is a bed first, and it sits like one. Before you order, measure the wall it will sit against, check the door swing, and remember that a day bed pushed against a wall needs its long side to the wall, not its end, or the back panel ends up in the middle of the room.

How much room does a trundle bed need to pull out?

About 190cm of clear floor beside the bed — the full length of the trundle itself. A side-pull trundle slides out sideways, so the floor beside the bed has to be empty when guests stay, which is the measurement people forget until the first sleepover.

This is the trundle's hidden cost, and it is the one that decides whether it works in your room. The trundle pulls out sideways, along its full length, so you need roughly 190cm of clear floor beside the bed — the same length as the bed itself — plus room to walk around it once it is out. If the room has a desk, a chest of drawers or a laundry pile along that wall, the trundle will not come out, and the second bed you paid for stays hidden. Some trundles pull out from the foot end instead, which needs less side clearance but more room at the foot of the bed, so check which way yours pulls before you plan the room around it. The lifting trundles are the exception worth knowing about: on models like the Stanley, the trundle rolls out and then rises on extendable legs to the same height as the main bed, so the two singles sit side by side as a double. That needs the same 190cm of floor, but the sleep is far better — two equal beds at the same height, rather than one bed at floor level and one at normal height. The trundle mattress rule

A trundle mattress has to be slim — usually 10 to 17cm deep, depending on the model — so it can slide back underneath the main bed. Check the maximum depth on the listing before you buy: a mattress that is too deep will not fit back under the frame, and the fix is a new mattress, not a new bed.

Which sleeps better — a day bed or a trundle bed?

The day bed, because it takes a full-depth single mattress of your choice. A trundle mattress has to be slim — usually 10 to 17cm deep — so it can slide back underneath, and a slim mattress on a low frame is fine for a night or two but not the sleep you would choose every night.

Comfort is where the two part company. The day bed sleeps like a normal single bed, because it is one: you choose the mattress, at whatever depth and firmness suits you, and it sits on a proper slatted base at normal bed height. A guest sleeping on a day bed gets the same sleep they would get at home, which is why day beds suit spare rooms where guests stay for several nights. The trundle is a different proposition: the mattress has to be slim to fit under the bed, and a slim mattress on a low frame at floor level is a compromise — fine for a child, fine for a night or two, but not the sleep an adult wants for a week. The lifting trundles close the gap, because the mattress rises to normal height and the sleeper is not on the floor, but the mattress is still slim, and slim means less comfort over several nights. The honest way to think about it is this: the day bed is a bed, and the trundle is a spare bed. If the room sleeps one person most nights and two occasionally, the day bed with a trundle is the right answer. If the room only ever sleeps guests, and never more than one at a time, a day bed on its own is enough.

Can adults sleep on a day bed or a trundle every night?

On a day bed, yes — it takes a standard single mattress, and adults sleep on it properly. On a trundle, only occasionally: the slim mattress and low frame are built for guests, and the weight limits tell the story, with some trundles rated to 100kg and sturdier ones to far more.

Adults can sleep on a day bed every night without compromise, because the bed is a standard single with a full-depth mattress — teenagers use them as their main bed, and adults use them in spare rooms and studios as a permanent bed that doubles as a sofa. The trundle is a different matter. The weight limits tell the story: a budget metal day bed with a trundle might rate the main bed at 150kg and the trundle at 100kg, which is fine for a child or a lighter adult but not for a heavier one, while a solid-wood trundle from a specialist maker can be rated to 474kg, which covers any adult comfortably. Check the rating on the listing, not the photo: a trundle that looks sturdy and a trundle that is sturdy are not the same thing. And remember the mattress depth rule — a heavier adult on a slim 10cm mattress will feel the slats through it, so a lifting trundle with a 17 to 20cm mattress is the better choice for adult guests. Trundle bed vs day bed: which should you buy?

Buy the day bed if the room needs seating by day and a proper bed at night; add the trundle if guests stay often. Buy a trundle under an ordinary single bed if the room is already a bedroom and you only need the second bed sometimes. The room's daytime job decides it.

The verdict is a use-case verdict, not a winner, because the two solve different problems and the right one depends on what the room does during the day. Buy the day bed if the spare room is also an office, a reading room or a teenager's bedroom — somewhere the bed needs to look like seating for most of the day and sleep someone properly at night. Add the trundle underneath if guests stay often, especially couples or two children, because one piece of furniture then sleeps two in the floor space of one. Buy a trundle under an ordinary single bed if the room is already a bedroom and you only need the second bed sometimes — for the grandchild who stays over, or the friend who visits twice a year — because the trundle disappears completely between visits and the room keeps its normal look. And if the room needs to sleep two people every night, buy neither: a day bed and a trundle are both guest solutions, and two permanent beds — a bunk, or two singles — will serve better. Trundle bed vs day bed FAQs

Is a trundle bed the same as a day bed?

No. A day bed is a single bed with a back and sides that works as seating by day. A trundle bed is a second, low bed on wheels that hides under another bed. Many products combine the two — a day bed with a trundle underneath — which is where the confusion comes from.

Do trundle beds come with mattresses?

Usually not. Most UK trundle beds are sold as frames only, and you buy two single mattresses separately — a full-depth one for the main bed and a slim one, 10 to 17cm deep, for the trundle. Check the listing before you order, because the trundle mattress depth is the detail that catches people out.

Can two adults sleep on a day bed with a trundle?

Yes, if both beds are full singles and the weight limits allow it. The main bed takes a standard single mattress, and a trundle that lifts to main-bed height can sit beside it as a second single. Check the trundle's weight rating first — some are built for children and rated to 100kg.

Is a day bed comfortable to sit on?

Comfortable enough for an evening, less so than a proper sofa. The seat is a mattress, so it sits higher and firmer than a sofa seat — around 76 to 83cm to the top of the frame — and the back is upright rather than reclined. Big scatter cushions along the back make a real difference.

What is better for a child's room, a trundle or a bunk bed?

A trundle, for younger children: there is no ladder, no top bunk and no fall risk, and the second bed only comes out when a friend stays. A bunk bed suits older children who want the top bunk and a room that needs two permanent beds — our guide to the best bunk beds for adults covers the weight and safety side.
