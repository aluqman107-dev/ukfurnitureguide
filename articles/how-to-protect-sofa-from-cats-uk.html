How to Protect a Sofa From Cats (UK 2026): Scratching, Fur and the Fixes That Work

Updated for 2026 · Independent advice — nobody pays for a place in this guide.

In short: cats scratch to keep their claws healthy and to mark territory, so scolding doesn't work — redirection does. Put a tall, stable scratching post right next to the sofa, cover the armrests they target, and choose tightly woven fabrics like microfibre or faux suede that claws can't hook into. The sofa survives; the cat keeps its claws.

Every cat owner knows the sound. That soft, rhythmic rasp from the living room, followed by the sight of a cat stretched full-length against the arm of a sofa that cost real money, digging in with every appearance of enjoyment. It is not spite. It is not bad behaviour. It is a cat doing exactly what cats have done for thousands of years — and the sooner you accept that, the sooner you can protect the sofa, because everything that works starts from understanding why they scratch, and everything that fails starts from trying to stop them scratching altogether.

Protecting a sofa from a cat means giving the cat something better to scratch, covering the surfaces it targets, and choosing fabrics its claws can't hook into — not punishing the cat for being a cat. Around 12 million UK households have a pet, roughly 44% of homes, according to the RSPCA, so this is not a niche problem. It is one of the most common reasons a good sofa gets ruined years early.

Why do cats scratch sofas?

Three reasons, and none of them is spite. Cats scratch to keep their claws sharp and healthy, to stretch the muscles in their shoulders and back, and to mark their territory — scratching leaves both a visual mark and a scent mark from glands in their paws, telling other cats this spot is taken.

That is why telling a cat off doesn't work, and why it never will. You are asking an animal to stop doing something its body is built to do, in the place it lives, for reasons it can't understand. The sofa is also, from a cat's point of view, the perfect scratching post: it is tall enough to stretch against, stable enough not to wobble, and it sits in the middle of the territory the cat cares about most — the living room, where its people are. A scratching post hidden in the spare room fails for the same reason a gym in the garage fails: it is in the wrong place. Cats scratch where they spend time, and they spend time on the sofa. The other thing to know is that scratching gets worse when a cat is bored or anxious — a new baby, a new cat, a house move, a change in routine — so a sudden increase in scratching is worth a thought about what changed, not just a new throw over the arm. If the behaviour changes sharply or comes with other signs of distress, a vet visit is worth it; the RSPCA's behaviour advice is a good first port of call.

What is the best sofa fabric for a home with cats?

Tightly woven fabrics their claws can't hook into: microfibre, faux suede and smooth faux leather. Loose weaves, bouclé and velvet are the worst — claws catch the loops, pull threads, and leave marks that no cleaning will fix.

Fabric choice is the single biggest factor in whether a sofa survives a cat, and it matters more than any cover or spray. The rule is simple: the tighter the weave, the safer the sofa. Microfibre is the standout — a tightly woven synthetic with no loops for claws to catch, which is also easy to wipe clean and quick to dry. Faux suede is nearly as good, designed to resist cat claws, and it has the soft feel that makes it a genuine alternative rather than a compromise. Smooth faux leather works too: claws slide off it rather than hooking in, and fur wipes off with a damp cloth — though a determined cat can still puncture it, and a puncture in faux leather is permanent. Genuine leather is tougher than faux but shows every scratch as a pale mark, so it is a mixed blessing. The fabrics to avoid are the ones with texture a claw can grab: loose weaves, bouclé with its looped pile, chenille, and velvet, which traps fur, shows scratches and crushes under claws. A fabric's durability is also measurable — the Martindale rub test rates upholstery fabric by how many rubs it survives, and anything over 20,000 rubs qualifies for domestic upholstery use; for a cat household, look for 30,000 or more. And remember that UK law requires upholstery fabric to be flame-retardant treated, so ask the retailer about the treatment if you buy from a small maker. How do I stop my cat scratching the sofa?

Redirect, don't punish. Put a tall, stable scratching post right beside the spot the cat scratches — tall enough for a full stretch, heavy enough not to wobble — and make the sofa itself unappealing with a cover or double-sided tape while the cat learns the new habit.

Redirection works because it gives the cat what it wants somewhere else. The post has to be right: tall enough for the cat to stretch fully — most cats need at least 75cm of vertical scratching surface — with a heavy, stable base that doesn't rock when they lean into it, and a material they like. Sisal rope is the favourite for most cats; carpet-covered posts are the worst, because the cat learns that carpet is for scratching and your carpet is carpet. Put the post touching the sofa, at the exact spot the cat uses, not across the room. When the cat uses it, praise it — a treat, a fuss, a calm voice. When it goes for the sofa, lift it gently to the post without a fuss; shouting teaches the cat to scratch when you're not looking, which is worse. While the habit forms, make the sofa boring to scratch: double-sided sticky tape on the armrests works because cats hate the sticky feeling on their paws, and a washable throw over the targeted area protects the fabric while the cat learns. Some cats dislike citrus scents, so a citrus-based spray on the fabric can help, but test it on a hidden patch first. And give the cat a reason to be elsewhere: a window seat, a cat tree near the radiator, a bed in the sunny spot — cats scratch the sofa partly because the sofa is where the good spots are. If the cat's own spot is better, the sofa stops being the destination. This pairs with the wider pet-proofing in our pet-owner sofa guide.

Scratch guards work — if you put them where the cat scratches

Clear adhesive scratch guards, stuck to the armrests and corners a cat targets, are the most reliable short-term protection there is: the cat can't hook its claws into smooth plastic, and most cats give up on that spot within days. The catch is placement — a guard on the front of the arm does nothing if the cat scratches the side. Watch where the cat actually scratches for a day, then cover exactly those spots.

Do sofa covers and scratch guards actually work?

Yes, with two conditions: the cover has to fit properly, and the guard has to be on the spot the cat actually uses. A loose throw slides off and gets scratched anyway; a fitted, washable cover or a clear adhesive guard on the right corner will take the damage instead of the sofa.

Covers are the honest answer for a sofa you already own and love. A fitted, washable cover — made to measure or a good stretch cover in the right size — protects the arms, the back and the seat, and it goes in the washing machine when the cat has been sick on it, which is a matter of when, not if. A loose throw is better than nothing, but it slides, bunches, and the cat scratches the bit that slipped off — which is always the bit you care about. The best covers are the ones you forget are there: a colour close to the sofa, a fabric that doesn't rustle, and a fit tight enough that the cat can't get a claw under the edge. Scratch guards are the targeted version: clear adhesive sheets, cut to size, stuck to the armrests, the sides and the corners — the places cats actually scratch. They are nearly invisible, they don't change how the sofa looks, and they are cheap enough to replace when they get scuffed. The combination is the strongest defence of all: guards on the corners, a fitted cover over the rest, and a scratching post beside the sofa for the cat to use instead. How do I deal with cat fur and smells on a sofa?

Rubber gloves, a lint roller, and bicarbonate of soda. Dampen a rubber glove and run your hand over the fabric — the fur balls up and lifts off in seconds. For smells, sprinkle bicarbonate of soda over the sofa, leave it for 30 minutes, then vacuum it away.

Fur is the daily tax of cat ownership, and the rubber-glove trick is the best answer anyone has found: a damp washing-up glove, run over the upholstery, collects fur into a neat pile that lifts straight off, and it works on microfibre, faux suede and most tight weaves where a lint roller just skates over the surface. A fabric shaver takes the pilling off the arms and seat edges, which makes a three-year-old sofa look a year younger. For smells, bicarbonate of soda is the honest fix — it absorbs odour without any liquid, and it costs pennies. Sprinkle it over the whole sofa, leave it for at least 30 minutes (overnight for strong smells), then vacuum it away with the upholstery attachment. The smell that won't shift with bicarb is usually in the filling, not the fabric, and that means a professional extraction clean — or, if the cat has been using the sofa as a toilet, an enzyme-based pet stain remover, which breaks down the compounds that cause the smell rather than masking them. Never use bleach on a fabric sofa, and never use a coloured cloth on a damp sofa — both leave marks worse than the problem. And keep the cat's own bed clean too: a washed cat bed means less fur and less smell transferred to the sofa in the first place.

Should I trim my cat's claws?

Yes, carefully — trimming the sharp tips every couple of weeks reduces the damage a scratch can do, and it is kind to the cat. Use proper cat nail clippers, cut only the clear tip, and stop well before the pink quick. Never declaw a cat: it is illegal in the UK except on veterinary advice for medical reasons.

Claw trimming is the most underused protection there is, because people are nervous of doing it wrong. The rule is simple: only the sharp, clear tip comes off — a millimetre or two — and you never cut into the pink part of the claw, the quick, which bleeds and hurts. If the cat won't sit still, do one paw at a time, over several days, with a treat after each one. A vet or a groomer will do it for a small fee if you'd rather not, and many will show you how the first time. What you must never do is declaw. Removing a cat's claws is illegal in the UK under the Animal Welfare Act 2006, except where a vet advises it for medical reasons, and for good reason: it is an amputation that changes how a cat walks, stretches and defends itself, and it often causes more behaviour problems than it solves. A trimmed claw, a tall post and a sofa in a claw-proof fabric will protect your furniture without ever needing to go near that line.

Protecting a sofa from cats FAQs

Why does my cat scratch the sofa?

Cats scratch to keep their claws sharp and healthy, to stretch their shoulder and back muscles, and to mark territory with both a visual scratch and a scent from glands in their paws. It is normal behaviour, not spite — which is why redirection to a scratching post works and punishment doesn't.

What fabric is best for a sofa with cats?

Tightly woven microfibre or faux suede — claws can't hook into the weave, and both clean easily. Smooth faux leather is also good, though it can puncture. Avoid velvet, bouclé and loose weaves: claws catch the loops, pull threads, and leave marks that won't clean out.

Do cat scratch guards work on sofas?

Yes — clear adhesive guards on the armrests and corners a cat actually targets are the most reliable short-term protection. The cat can't hook its claws into smooth plastic and usually gives up on that spot within days. They only fail when they're placed where the cat doesn't scratch.

How do I get cat fur off a sofa?

Dampen a rubber washing-up glove and run your hand over the fabric — the fur balls up and lifts off in seconds. A lint roller works on smooth fabrics, and a fabric shaver takes the pilling off the arms and seat edges. Vacuum with the upholstery attachment weekly.

Is it cruel to stop a cat scratching?

Stopping a cat from scratching entirely would be — scratching is a natural, necessary behaviour. The kind approach is redirection: a tall, stable scratching post beside the sofa, claw trimming, and protecting the sofa with guards or a cover, so the cat keeps its claws and the sofa keeps its fabric.
