How to Stop a Headboard Banging Against the Wall (UK 2026): 7 Fixes That Work

Updated for 2026 · Independent advice — nobody pays for a place in this guide.

In short: a banging headboard is almost always loose bolts, a gap to the wall, or a bed sliding on a hard floor — and it is usually fixed in an evening. Tighten the bolts first, then stick felt pads or rubber bumpers on the back of the headboard, then stop the bed sliding with grip pads under the feet. Only a gap wider than about 6mm needs a stabiliser, and only a cracked headboard needs replacing.

It starts quietly. A soft knock when you turn over, so faint you are not sure you heard it. Then it gets louder — a thud every time someone sits on the bed, a rattle when the dog jumps up, a bang in the middle of the night that wakes the person in the next room. A headboard banging against the wall is one of those small household noises that grows until it fills the house, and it is also one of the easiest to fix, because the cause is almost always one of three simple things, and all three have cheap fixes.

A headboard bangs against the wall when the bed moves and the headboard has room to travel — either because its bolts have loosened, because there is a gap between the headboard and the wall, or because the whole bed slides on a hard floor. This guide works through the causes in order, cheapest fix first, so you can find yours in an evening and fix it the same night.

Why does my headboard bang against the wall?

Because something in the bed has room to move. Either the bolts holding the headboard to the frame have worked loose, so the headboard rocks; or there is a gap between the headboard and the wall, so the whole bed shifts and the headboard hits the plaster; or the bed slides on a hard floor every time someone moves.

Those three causes cover almost every banging headboard, and they are easy to tell apart once you know what to look for. Loose bolts are the commonest: a headboard is held to the bed frame by bolts and barrel nuts, and months of vibration — every turn, every sit-down, every time someone leans back against it — work them loose a fraction of a millimetre at a time, until the headboard has enough play to rock. A gap is the second cause: most beds sit a few millimetres from the wall because of the skirting board, and if the bed shifts even slightly, the headboard crosses that gap and hits the plaster. And a sliding bed is the third: on wood, laminate or tile, a bed with bare feet creeps a little every time someone gets in or out, and each creep ends with the headboard tapping the wall. The three causes often combine — a bed that slides makes its bolts loosen faster, and loose bolts let the headboard travel further — which is why the fix usually takes two steps rather than one.

How do I find out what is causing the banging?

Push the headboard with your hand. If it rocks side to side, the bolts are loose. If the headboard is solid but the whole bed moves, the bed is sliding on the floor. If the bed is solid and the headboard is solid, measure the gap to the wall — anything over about 6mm is enough to make a noise.

Diagnose before you buy anything, because the fix depends on the cause and the wrong fix wastes money. Start with the push test: stand at the side of the bed, put both hands on the headboard, and push it gently from side to side, then forwards and back. If it rocks or rattles, the connection between the headboard and the frame is loose — that is your cause, and tightening the bolts will likely end the noise. If the headboard is solid but the whole bed shifts when you push it, the bed is sliding on the floor, and grip pads under the feet are the fix. If neither moves, the problem is the gap: pull the bed out a few centimetres, measure the distance from the back of the headboard to the wall, and look at the skirting board — in many UK homes the skirting holds the bed 15 to 20mm off the wall, which is more than enough room for a bang. A gap wider than about 6mm usually needs a stabiliser rather than pads, because pads can only absorb a knock, not close a gap. And while you are there, check the floor: a bed on an uneven floor rocks on two legs, which loosens bolts faster than anything else.

The 6mm rule

A gap of about 6mm — a quarter of an inch — between the headboard and the wall is enough to make a noise, and it is the point where felt pads stop being enough. Below 6mm, pads or bumpers will absorb the knock. Above it, fit a threaded stabiliser that presses the headboard firmly against the wall instead.

Will tightening the bolts fix a banging headboard?

Usually, yes — and it is the first thing to try. Headboard bolts work loose over months of vibration, and a quarter-turn with the right spanner or Allen key often ends the noise completely. Check every bolt where the headboard meets the frame rails, on both sides, and tighten them in turn.

Start here, because it costs nothing and it fixes most cases. Pull the bed away from the wall, find the bolts where the headboard posts meet the frame rails — usually two on each side, sometimes hidden behind plastic caps — and check each one with the right tool, which is usually a spanner or an Allen key that came with the bed. Tighten them in turn, a quarter-turn at a time, going around the bed rather than tightening one side fully before the other, so the headboard seats evenly. If a bolt turns freely without tightening, the hole is stripped: the wood or the thread has worn, and tightening alone will not hold. In that case, fit a heavy-duty steel mending plate or an L-bracket across the joint, screwed into sound wood on both sides, which bridges the worn area and locks the joint solid again. A slightly larger bolt with a washer can do the same job by spreading the load over fresh material. And once the bolts are tight, check them again in a month — a bolt that has worked loose once will do it again, and a quick check every few months keeps the noise from coming back.

What should I put behind a headboard to stop the noise?

Thick felt pads or rubber bumpers stuck to the back of the headboard where it meets the wall. They absorb the knock instead of passing it into the plaster, and they protect the paintwork too. For a bigger gap, a foam tube or a tightly rolled towel wedged behind the headboard does the same job as a stopgap.

Pads and bumpers are the quickest fix there is, and they do two jobs at once: they silence the knock and they protect the wall. Thick adhesive felt pads — the kind sold for the bottom of chair legs — stick to the back of the headboard at the contact points, usually the top corners and the centre, and they absorb the impact so the knock becomes a soft thud or nothing at all. Dense rubber bumpers, sometimes sold as door stoppers, do the same job more firmly and last longer, which matters on a heavy upholstered headboard that hits the wall with real force. For a bigger gap, a foam tube cut into rings and glued to the back of the headboard works as a cushion, and a tightly rolled towel or a piece of dense foam wedged behind the headboard does the same job as a stopgap while you wait for a proper fix. The trick with all of these is placement: put the padding exactly where the headboard touches the wall, not where it looks tidy, and use enough of it — two pads on a wide headboard will still let the middle bang. And remember what pads cannot do: they absorb a knock, they do not stop the movement, so if the bolts are loose, tighten those first and use the pads as the second step, not the only one.

Do headboard stabilisers work?

Yes — they are the proper fix for a gap that pads alone cannot close. A stabiliser is a threaded rod with a rubber foot that screws into the back of the headboard and extends until the pad presses firmly against the wall, holding the headboard still. Fit one near each top corner, where the leverage is greatest.

Stabilisers — sold as anti-shake tools or bed buffers — are the fix the other methods lead up to, and they work because they remove the gap entirely rather than cushioning it. The device is a threaded rod with a soft rubber foot on one end: you screw or stick the base into the back of the headboard, usually near the top corners where the leverage is greatest, then turn the rod to extend it until the rubber foot presses firmly against the wall. Once both sides are pressing, the headboard cannot move backwards at all, because the stabiliser is holding it still — the bed would have to push the wall to move. They are cheap, they need no drilling into the wall, and they work on any gap from a few millimetres to several centimetres, which is why they are the right answer for a bed held off the wall by deep skirting. The one thing to watch is the wall surface: the rubber foot presses against the plaster, so on a freshly painted wall it can leave a mark over time — put a small felt pad between the foot and the wall if that worries you. And fit them in pairs: one stabiliser stops the rocking on its own side, but the other side will still move.

Should I fix my headboard to the wall?

If you own the home and the banging keeps coming back, yes — it is the most permanent fix. A French cleat or a pair of flush-mount brackets screwed into the wall studs locks the headboard in place completely. Renters should skip this one: it means drilling, and the landlord will not thank you for it.

Wall-fixing is the last resort and the most permanent one, and it is worth doing properly if you go this far. A French cleat is two interlocking metal brackets: one screws to the back of the headboard, the other screws to the wall, ideally into the studs — the vertical timbers inside a plasterboard wall — and the headboard then slides down onto the wall bracket and locks in place, unable to move in any direction. Flush-mount brackets work the same way in a smaller package. The key step is finding the studs: a stud finder is a few pounds from a hardware shop, and it saves you from screwing into plasterboard, which will not hold a headboard. In a solid brick wall, use wall plugs and screws rated for the weight. The renter's version of this fix is the stabiliser, which needs no wall fixings at all — it screws into the headboard and presses against the wall, so it leaves nothing behind when you move out. And one warning: never fix a headboard to the wall as a substitute for tightening loose bolts. A headboard that is loose on its frame will still rattle against its own brackets, and you will have drilled holes for nothing.

How do I stop the whole bed sliding?

Put grip under the feet. Rubber furniture pads or non-slip leg covers under each leg stop a bed sliding on wood, laminate or tile, and furniture cups do the same job on carpet by spreading the weight and stopping castors from rolling. Clean the feet first, or the adhesive will not hold.

A sliding bed is a common cause of a banging headboard, and it is also the easiest to fix, because the fix is friction. On hard floors — wood, laminate, tile — the bed's feet have almost nothing to grip, so every time someone gets in or out, the bed creeps a few millimetres, and each creep ends with the headboard tapping the wall. Rubber furniture pads stuck under each leg add the grip the floor is missing, and non-slip leg covers do the same job while protecting the floor from scratches. On carpet, the problem is different: the bed's feet sink into the pile, and castors — the little wheels some divans sit on — roll. Furniture cups, made of dense plastic or silicone, sit under each foot and spread the weight over a wider area, which stops the feet sinking and the castors rolling. Whichever you use, clean the feet first: dust and old polish stop adhesive pads from sticking, and a pad that falls off in a week is worse than no pad at all. Lift one corner of the bed at a time — get someone to help, because a made-up double bed is heavy — and stick the pad to the bottom of the foot, not to the floor, so it moves with the bed.

When is a headboard beyond fixing?

When the bolt holes are stripped and the wood around them is split, or the headboard itself is cracked through. A mending plate can save a stripped hole once, but a split post or a cracked panel will keep moving whatever you do. At that point, buy a new bed.

Most headboards are fixable, and the fixes in this guide cover the vast majority — but some are not, and it is worth knowing the difference before you spend money on parts. A stripped bolt hole can be saved once with a mending plate or a larger bolt, but if the wood around the hole is split, the joint has lost its strength and no bracket will hold it for long. A headboard panel cracked through the middle, or a post snapped at the joint, is the same story: the structure is broken, and the headboard will keep moving whatever you fit to it. The other sign is age: a headboard that has been tightened, padded and stabilised and still bangs is telling you the frame itself has had it, and the money is better spent on a replacement. If you do replace the bed, choose one with a headboard that bolts solidly to the frame rather than slotting in — a bolted headboard is the one that stays quiet — and check the fixings before the delivery men leave. Headboard banging FAQs

Why does my headboard bang when I move in bed?

Because the bed moves and the headboard has room to travel before it hits the wall. The usual causes are bolts that have worked loose, a gap between the headboard and the wall, or a bed sliding on a hard floor. Push the headboard by hand first — if it rocks, tighten the bolts; if the whole bed moves, grip the feet.

Will felt pads stop a headboard banging?

Yes, for light knocking — thick felt pads or rubber bumpers stuck to the back of the headboard absorb the impact and protect the wall. They will not fix a headboard that rocks on loose bolts or a gap wider than about 6mm, which needs tightening or a stabiliser instead.

Can I stop a headboard banging without drilling?

Yes. Tighten the bolts, stick felt pads or rubber bumpers on the back of the headboard, and put non-slip pads under the bed legs. Threaded stabilisers also need no wall fixings — they screw into the headboard itself and press against the wall. Only a French cleat needs drilling.

Why does my bed keep moving away from the wall?

Because the feet have nothing to grip. On wood, laminate or tile, bare feet slide a little every time someone gets in or out of bed. Rubber furniture pads under each leg add the friction the floor is missing, and furniture cups do the same job on carpet.

Is a banging headboard damaging my wall?

Yes, slowly. Every knock chips paint and dents plaster, and over months it leaves a mark exactly where the headboard hits. Felt pads or rubber bumpers stop the damage as well as the noise, which is why they are worth fitting even if the banging does not bother you.
