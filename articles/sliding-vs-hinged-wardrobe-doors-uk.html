Sliding vs Hinged Wardrobe Doors: Which Is Best for a UK Bedroom? (2026)

Updated for 2026 · Independent advice — nobody pays for a place in this guide.

In short: sliding doors win in tight bedrooms because they need no swing space; hinged doors win when you want to see everything at once, have an awkward room shape, or want the cheapest durable option. The deciding measurement is simple: if you have less than 60–80cm clear in front of the wardrobe, sliding is the practical answer. If you have the space, hinged is usually the better wardrobe.

Wardrobe doors are one of those choices that looks like a style decision and turns out to be a space decision. You open them every day, in the dark, half awake, and the wrong type will annoy you twice daily for a decade. The right type disappears into the routine. This guide compares the two properly — not just the showroom pitch for each, but the track cleaning, the hinge adjustments, the alcove that defeats one type entirely, and the box room where the other is the only sane option.

A sliding wardrobe door is a door that hangs from, or runs along, a horizontal track and glides sideways in front of its neighbour, so it never swings into the room. A hinged wardrobe door is a conventional door on hinges that swings outward toward you, opening the full width of the wardrobe at once. That one mechanical difference — sideways versus outward — decides almost everything that follows.

Sliding vs hinged wardrobe doors: how do they compare?

Sliding doors need no clearance in front of the wardrobe, look modern and suit mirrored panels, but only open half the wardrobe at a time and cost more. Hinged doors open the whole interior at once, cost less and last longer with less fuss, but each door needs 45–60cm of swing space and will hit the bed in a tight room.

Which wardrobe door type saves the most space?

Sliding, by a clear margin — it saves the entire swing area, which in a typical UK box room is 0.5–0.8 square metres of floor you get back. That is the difference between a bedside table fitting or not, and between walking past the wardrobe sideways or normally.

Do the bedroom maths before anything else. A standard double bed is 135cm wide, and you want at least 60cm of walkway beside it — 75cm is comfortable. If the wardrobe faces the bed, hinged doors swinging toward the bed eat that walkway entirely, and you end up shuffling sideways every morning. Sliding doors leave the walkway untouched. The same logic applies to wardrobes beside a bedroom door: a hinged door that swings into the doorway's arc is a daily collision. That said, sliding only saves space in front of the wardrobe. The wardrobe itself is usually deeper — the overlapping track system adds a few centimetres of depth — so in a very shallow alcove, a hinged wardrobe can actually be the slimmer unit overall. Measure the alcove depth as well as the floor space.

Is it annoying only seeing half your wardrobe at a time?

For some people, yes — genuinely, daily. With sliding doors you can only ever see one section, so finding the right shirt means sliding, looking, sliding back. If you share the wardrobe, one person's section is always behind the other's door. Hinged doors show you everything in one glance, which is faster and calmer on a rushed morning.

Whether it bothers you depends on how you organise clothes. People who organise by section — work clothes one side, casual the other — barely notice, because they only ever open one side. People who browse, or who share with a partner whose side is always the hidden one, notice it every day. There is a middle ground: three-door sliding wardrobes, where the middle panel slides either way, reduce the problem without removing it. Replacement sliding wardrobe door rollers on Amazon UK are also widely available if an older door starts to drag, which takes some of the sting out of the maintenance question. And interior layout matters as much as doors — a well-planned interior with drawers and shelving at the front reduces the need to see everything at once. If you are the kind of person who stands in front of an open wardrobe deciding what to wear, hinged doors are worth the floor space. If you know what you're grabbing, sliding doors won't slow you down at all.

Which lasts longer: sliding or hinged wardrobe doors?

Hinged doors, on average, with less maintenance along the way. A hinge is a simple piece of hardware: tighten a screw every few years and it carries on. A sliding system has tracks, rollers, guides and alignment — more parts, more that can drift out of true, and a door that starts sticking is a door you start avoiding.

That is the honest durability picture, with one big caveat: quality dominates type. A well-made sliding wardrobe from a proper fitted-bedroom company, installed by someone who measured twice, will outlast a cheap flat-pack hinged wardrobe whose hinges are screwed into chipboard. The failure modes are what differ. Sliding doors fail by sticking, jumping the track, or scraping — usually fixable by cleaning the track and adjusting the rollers, which is a 20-minute job once you know how. Hinged doors fail by dropping — the door sags, catches the frame, and won't close — fixed by adjusting the hinge screws, or fitting soft-close wardrobe hinges on Amazon UK if you want the upgrade while you're at it, which is a five-minute job. In a house with uneven floors or walls that aren't quite square, which describes a large share of British housing stock, hinged doors cope better because each door is adjusted independently. Sliding tracks demand level and square, and old houses rarely offer either.

The track-cleaning reality

Sliding wardrobe tracks collect dust, carpet fluff and the occasional stray sock. Vacuum the bottom track monthly and wipe the rollers, and the doors will glide for years. Ignore it for a year and the doors start to judder, which most people blame on the wardrobe when it is really just housekeeping. If you hate fiddly maintenance, that monthly two minutes is a genuine reason to choose hinged.

How much more do sliding wardrobe doors cost?

Expect sliding to cost roughly 20–40% more than an equivalent hinged wardrobe, because of the track system, the larger panels and the more precise installation. For a fitted run, the gap is often a few hundred pounds; for a freestanding wardrobe, sliding versions are rarer and the premium can be steeper.

The price gap is not just the doors. Sliding wardrobes usually need professional measuring and fitting — the tracks have to be level to the millimetre, and a DIY job that is slightly out will stick forever. Hinged wardrobes are far more forgiving to fit and to adjust afterwards, which is why they dominate the freestanding market and the flat-pack market. There is also a hidden cost on the sliding side: replacement rollers and track parts are specific to the system, so in ten years you may be hunting for a part from a company that has changed its range. Hinges are standard, cheap and available in every hardware shop in the country. If budget is the deciding factor and the room allows it, hinged is the better value — spend the saving on the interior fittings instead, which is where daily satisfaction actually comes from.

Do sliding wardrobe doors work in alcoves and sloping ceilings?

Badly, in both cases — and this is the reason to rule them out early if either applies. Sliding tracks need a straight, level run and full-height clearance across the whole width. A sloping ceiling means the doors can't be full height, an uneven floor means the track won't run true, and a chimney-breast alcove usually means the opening isn't wide enough for two overlapping panels to be useful.

Hinged doors are the alcove answer. Each door is sized to its opening, hinges adjust for the wonk, and a door that is slightly narrower than its neighbour still opens and closes properly. The same goes for period properties with picture rails, coving and skirting that eat into the opening — hinged doors can be trimmed and adjusted around them, sliding doors cannot. One exception: a very wide, straight, modern wall with no obstructions is sliding's ideal home, and a floor-to-ceiling mirrored sliding run there genuinely transforms a room, bouncing light around in a way hinged doors never will. If your bedroom has that wall, sliding earns its premium. If it doesn't, don't force it.

Sliding vs hinged: which should renters choose?

Hinged, freestanding — every time. Renters need furniture that assembles inside the room, comes apart for the next move, and needs no wall fixing. That is a hinged freestanding wardrobe by definition; sliding wardrobes are overwhelmingly fitted, heavy, and a liability when the tenancy ends.

The renter's version of this decision is really about the wardrobe, not just the doors. A freestanding hinged wardrobe goes up in the bedroom, comes down in pieces, and fits in a van. A fitted sliding wardrobe belongs to the property — and in a rental, you would need the landlord's written permission to install it, and you would be leaving it behind. If you love the sliding look in a rental, the compromise is a freestanding wardrobe with sliding doors, which does exist, but check the weight and the depth carefully, and be honest about whether you'll ever want to move it. What mistakes do people make choosing wardrobe doors?

  • Measuring the wall but not the floor space — the wardrobe fits, but its doors can't open. Measure swing space, not just width.
  • Forgetting the ceiling — coving, picture rails and sloping ceilings all steal height from sliding doors. Measure at both ends, not just the middle.
  • Choosing mirrored sliding doors for a dark room — mirrors bounce light, but they also bounce the mess. A mirrored wardrobe facing an unmade bed doubles the clutter visually.
  • Ignoring the interior — doors get the attention, but drawers, shelves and hanging space decide whether you enjoy the wardrobe. Budget for the inside too.
  • Buying fitted for a rental — fitted wardrobes stay with the property. In a rental, freestanding is the only honest choice.
  • Skipping the track-cleaning question — if you won't vacuum a track monthly, don't buy sliding doors. It really is that simple.

One last practical point, whichever type you choose: put the wardrobe where the doors open onto clear floor, not onto the bed, the radiator or the bedroom door. A wardrobe that fights the room's circulation will irritate you long after you've forgotten what it cost. Wardrobe door FAQs

Are sliding wardrobe doors better for small bedrooms?

Yes, usually — because they need no swing space, they give back 0.5–0.8 square metres of floor in a typical box room. The exception is a very shallow alcove, where the deeper track system can make a sliding wardrobe the bulkier unit overall. Measure both the floor space and the alcove depth.

Can you see the whole wardrobe with sliding doors?

No — only half at a time, since the doors overlap. People who organise clothes by section rarely mind, but if you browse the whole wardrobe to choose an outfit, or share with a partner, hinged doors showing everything at once will suit you better.

Do sliding wardrobe doors break easily?

Not easily, but they need more care than hinged doors. The common problems are sticking tracks and doors drifting out of alignment, both usually fixed by cleaning the track and adjusting the rollers. Hinges are simpler: an occasional screw-tightening is the whole maintenance routine.

Are hinged wardrobe doors cheaper than sliding?

Yes — typically 20–40% cheaper for an equivalent wardrobe, because the hardware is simpler and installation is more forgiving. Sliding wardrobes usually need precise professional fitting, while hinged wardrobes are far more DIY-friendly and their hinges are cheap standard parts.

Can sliding wardrobe doors fit a sloping ceiling?

Poorly. Sliding tracks need a straight, level run and full-height clearance across the whole width, which a sloping ceiling prevents. Hinged doors cope far better with slopes, uneven floors and alcoves, because each door is sized and adjusted independently.
