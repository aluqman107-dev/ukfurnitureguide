Metal vs Wooden Bed Frame: Honest UK Comparison (2026)

Updated for 2026 · Independent advice — nobody pays for a place in this guide.

In short: there is no overall winner — only the right frame for your life. Renting, moving flats, tight stairs? Metal: lighter, cheaper, packs flatter. Light sleeper, want something that feels like furniture forever? Solid wood: quieter, warmer, more substantial. Damp room? Metal with its coating intact, or well-finished hardwood — never raw pine. Want the look? Metal suits minimal and Victorian styles; wood suits warm, classic and Scandi rooms.

Walk into any UK bed retailer and the first fork in the road is the same: metal or wood. The choice follows you for years — it decides whether your bed creaks when you turn over, whether you can carry it down two flights of stairs, and whether it still looks respectable after five years of daily use.

Definitions first. A metal bed frame is a bed frame whose structure — headboard, footboard, rails and slats — is made from metal tubing or bars, most often steel, sometimes iron or aluminium, usually finished with a powder-coated surface that seals it against rust. (Metal is not one material: steel is the standard, iron frames are heavier and often decorative in Victorian style, aluminium is lighter and will not rust but costs more.) A wooden bed frame is the same job done in timber: solid hardwood like oak at the top end, pine in the middle, MDF or veneered chipboard at the budget end. Both are the skeleton your mattress sits on — and the skeleton decides a surprising amount about how you sleep.

This guide is written for the UK reality: renting is common, flats are small, stairs are narrow, and damp is a genuine issue. There is no fake overall winner here — just the honest trade-off on noise, durability, weight, rust, looks and cost, and a verdict by lifestyle at the end.

Which is more durable, a metal or wooden bed frame?

Both can last a decade or more, but they age differently. A solid hardwood frame stays rigid and quiet and shrugs off years of use; a well-made metal frame — welded rather than bolted together — is extremely strong for its weight, but its joints loosen over time, and loose joints are where creaking and wobble begin.

Durability is where the marketing gets loudest. Steel is, pound for pound, stronger than wood. But bed frames do not fail because the material gives up; they fail at the joints. A bolted metal frame has a dozen joints that shift a fraction with every movement, and over months that loosens bolts and chips the coating. A wooden frame's joints move far less, because wood absorbs and dampens movement rather than transmitting it.

The material quality spectrum matters more than the metal-versus-wood question itself. A solid oak frame from a proper joiner will outlive everyone reading this; a thin flat-pack steel frame with wobbly slat holders may not see its fifth birthday. If durability is your top priority, judge the individual frame — its joints, its slats, its centre rail — before you judge the material. Our bed buying guide walks through exactly what to check.

Do metal bed frames squeak?

They can, and it is the single most common complaint about them. The squeak comes from joints and bolts working loose over time, not from the metal itself. The fix is unglamorous but effective: tighten every bolt, add felt pads or PTFE tape where tubes slot together, and recheck after a month once the new frame has settled.

Here is the honest physics. Metal transmits vibration; wood absorbs it. When you roll over, a wooden frame soaks up the energy through the grain and the mass of the timber. A metal frame carries that energy to every joint, and any joint with a hair of slack becomes a little speaker — which is why a new metal frame is usually silent and a two-year-old one sometimes is not.

Before you write metal off, know that it is almost always fixable — a ten-minute job, not a new bed. Work through the frame with a spanner or Allen key and snug every fixing, especially the centre rail and slat holders. Stick felt pads where metal meets metal at the joints, or wrap PTFE plumber's tape around the slat pins. Wooden frames can creak too, but it happens far less often.

The noise hierarchy, honestly stated

Quietest: solid hardwood frame with good joints. Next: well-made pine or bolted metal frame, properly tightened. Noisiest: cheap bolted metal frames with thin slat holders and no centre rail. One caveat: divan bases are quieter than all of them — if noise is your only worry, see our sleigh bed vs divan comparison.

Which bed frame is better for renters?

Metal, in most cases. Renting means moving, and moving means stairs: a metal double frame is typically light enough for one person to manage, flat-packs into a compact box, and reassembles in under an hour with basic tools. A solid wooden frame is a two-person job at best — and a professional removal job at worst.

Timber is dense. A hardwood double frame is genuinely heavy — awkward in stairwells and a deposit-risk when it scuffs the walls going round the landing. This is the angle retailers rarely talk about, but anyone who has moved house with a solid oak king-size frame will tell you: the weight is not a minor detail.

Metal wins the logistics game three ways: it is lighter, it flat-packs smaller — most metal frames arrive in one manageable box — and it assembles faster with fewer, simpler fixings. For a renter on a twelve-month tenancy who might be moving again next summer, that matters.

Is a metal or wooden frame better for a heavy person?

Both are fine if you buy well, but the checklist is different. For metal, look for welded joints, a solid centre rail and a stated maximum weight that comfortably covers both sleepers; for wood, look for thick hardwood rails and plenty of slats — slat count and centre-rail support matter more than the species of timber.

The worry behind this question is real: nobody wants a frame that sags. The reassuring truth is that any decent frame handles two adults easily. Where heavy sleepers get into trouble is with cheap frames of either type: thin tubular steel with a flimsy centre rail, or soft pine with slats spaced too far apart. On anything double-size or bigger, insist on a proper centre rail with support legs and a stated maximum weight that covers both sleepers.

Do metal bed frames rust?

In a normal dry UK bedroom, rust is not a realistic worry. Almost every metal bed frame sold in the UK is powder-coated — the metal is sprayed with a dry powder and baked, which fuses into a hard, even shell that seals it against everyday moisture. Problems start only where that shell is broken: a chip down to bare metal, or a frame living in a genuinely damp room.

Powder-coating is genuinely good — the same finish used on outdoor furniture and white goods — but it is not armour. Drag a frame across a hard floor, or crack the coating around a bolt hole, and bare steel meets air. In a dry bedroom that barely matters; in a room with condensation running down the windows, it is where rust gets its foothold.

The maintenance is simple: touch up chips with matching enamel paint, keep the room ventilated, and don't wedge the frame against a damp external wall. Wood has its own damp story: untreated pine in a humid room can go mouldy or warp, so hardwood or a properly finished frame is the safer wooden choice. If your bedroom has a real damp problem, fix the damp — neither material forgives it.

Which looks better — metal or wood?

Neither — it is entirely about the room. Metal suits minimal, industrial and Victorian styles, from sleek black tube frames to ornate iron designs with brass details; wood brings warmth, suits classic, Scandi and cottage rooms, and photographs as solid furniture. Pick the one that flatters your room, not the one the internet tells you to.

Style is where people get most dogmatic and the answer is most personal. Metal frames visually disappear: thin black or white tubes take up almost no visual space, which suits small bedrooms and guest rooms. Ornate iron frames are the opposite — a statement piece, magnificent in a period property with high ceilings, less so in a low-ceilinged box room. Wood brings mass and warmth: an oak frame anchors a room and says furniture in a way a thin metal frame never will.

And if you like to redecorate often, black metal goes with everything; a cherry-stained pine frame has opinions.

The wooden case, made properly

Wood's pitch is simple: it feels like furniture. Solid timber has a weight and warmth that makes a bedroom feel settled — a proper hardwood frame does not wobble, does not rattle, and looks better in ten years than the day it arrived.

The spectrum within wood is enormous. Hardwood — oak, ash, walnut — is the top tier: dense, stable, built to last decades. Pine and rubberwood are the honest middle: lighter and kinder on the wallet, but softer. Then the budget tier to watch: MDF and veneered chipboard frames, which look like wood but behave nothing like it — heavy, vulnerable to damp, unforgiving if you strip a screw. Cheap for a reason.

If you want the full furniture feel — headboard with presence, rails you could sit a toddler on, timber that takes a polish — wood is the material, and hardwood is the wood. The verdict — by lifestyle, not a fake winner

Still on the fence? Match yourself to the row that fits.

  • Renting or moving within a year or two: metal. Lighter, cheaper, flat-packs, assembles fast — and you will not mourn it if the next flat has different stairs.
  • Light sleeper or shift worker: solid wood. The quietest frames in this guide are wooden, and a creaking bed at 3am is a special kind of misery.
  • Tight budget, first bed: metal. A basic steel frame is one of the cheapest ways into a proper bed — spend what you save on a better mattress, which affects your sleep far more.
  • Settled in your own home: hardwood. Buy once, build once, forget about it for a decade — this is where wood's longevity pays off.
  • Damp or poorly ventilated room: powder-coated metal with the coating intact, or well-finished hardwood. Skip raw pine and chipped frames until the damp is fixed.
  • Small bedroom or guest room: metal. Thin frames visually disappear and leave more room for the door to open.

And one parting truth that applies to both: the mattress matters more than the frame. A great mattress on a basic frame sleeps better than a cheap mattress on a beautiful one. Get the frame right for your life, then put the real money where your back meets it.

What to avoid

  • Any double+ frame with no centre rail — the middle is where frames sag. Non-negotiable, metal or wooden.
  • Thin bolted metal frames with wobbly slat holders — the classic creaker. If the listing photos show flimsy fixings, believe them.
  • MDF/chipboard sold as a "wooden bed" — it swells with damp and strips its screws. Check the specification, not the headline.
  • Raw unfinished pine in a damp room — it can warp and go mouldy. Hardwood or a properly finished frame only.
  • Buying on looks alone — the prettiest frame is worthless if the joints, slats and weight rating are poor. Check the specification first, the style second.

Metal vs wooden bed frame FAQs

Do metal bed frames squeak?

They can. Squeaking in metal frames almost always comes from joints and bolts working loose over time, not from the metal itself. The fix is simple: tighten every bolt, and add felt pads or a little PTFE tape where tubes slot together. Check them again after a month, since new frames settle.

Is a wooden or metal bed frame more durable?

Both can last a decade or more, but they age differently. A solid hardwood frame stays rigid and quiet and shrugs off years of use. A good metal frame — welded rather than bolted — is very strong but will eventually loosen at its joints, which is when the creaking starts.

Can a metal bed frame rust in a UK bedroom?

Most metal bed frames sold in the UK are powder-coated, which seals the metal against everyday moisture, so rust is rare in a normal dry bedroom. Problems start in damp rooms or where the coating is chipped down to bare metal — scratches should be touched up to keep the protection intact.

Is a metal bed frame strong enough for two adults?

Yes, provided it is well made. Steel bed frames are very strong for their weight, and a decent one comfortably supports two adults. What matters is the construction: welded joints and a solid centre rail beat a thin bolted frame, and you should always check the manufacturer's stated maximum weight.

Which is better for a small flat — a metal or wooden bed frame?

Metal, usually. A metal frame is lighter and packs flat, so it is easier to carry up stairs and assemble solo in a small flat. Wooden frames are heavier and bulkier to move, and can need more care in humid or poorly ventilated rooms.
